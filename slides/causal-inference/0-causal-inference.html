CAUSAL INFERENCE

Hootsworth: Numbers tell you what happened. Causal inference tells you why. Every time you ask "did this change actually make a difference?" — that is a causal question. This deck gives you the rigorous toolkit to answer it.

WHAT IS CAUSAL INFERENCE?

Causal inference is the branch of statistics dedicated to answering one type of question: "Did X actually cause Y — or did they just happen at the same time?"

Seeing two things move together (correlation) is not the same as knowing one caused the other (causation). Ice cream sales and drowning deaths both rise in summer — not because ice cream is dangerous, but because hot weather drives both. Causal inference finds the real driver.

The core challenge: you can never observe the same unit in two states at once. You can't show the same customer both the old and new website simultaneously. The missing "what would have happened without the change?" is called the counterfactual. Every method in this deck is a different strategy for constructing it.

WHERE IT SHOWS UP

DomainCausal question
Digital / e-commerce Did the website redesign increase checkout conversions — or would sales have grown anyway due to seasonal demand? Did the free delivery promo drive revenue, or were customers already planning to buy?
Healthcare Does this drug reduce hospital readmissions — or do healthier patients happen to choose it? (If doctors prescribe it to lower-risk patients, a naive comparison will flatter the drug.)
Public policy Did raising the minimum wage cause unemployment to rise — or were those regions already economically struggling before the law passed?

THE FUNDAMENTAL PROBLEM

Every causal question boils down to one equation:

Causal Effect = Yobserved(with intervention) − Ycounterfactual(without intervention)

The counterfactual is never observed. You only ever see the world with the intervention. The entire field of causal inference is about constructing the missing second term.

EVERY METHOD = A DIFFERENT CONSTRUCTION STRATEGY

StrategyHow it builds the counterfactual
Randomized Controlled Trial (A/B test) You create a control group by randomly splitting users. The control group's outcome is the counterfactual. Randomization ensures the two groups are identical in expectation — so any difference after the change is causal.
Difference-in-Differences (DiD) You find an existing untreated group (e.g. a different region). Compare how both groups changed before vs. after — the double difference removes shared trends.
Synthetic Control (SCM) You mix several untreated places into one fake "twin" that matched the treated place before the intervention. That twin is the counterfactual.
Interrupted Time Series (ITS) No control group at all. You use the treated unit's own history to learn its normal pattern, then project that pattern forward. The gap between projection and reality = the causal effect.
► The method you pick depends entirely on what data structure you have — not on which Python library looks nicest.

THE DECISION TREE

Two branching questions determine your method:

Can you randomize?
├─ YES → Randomized Controlled Trial (A/B Test)
└─ NO → Do you have an untreated control group?
├─ YES → One donor tracks treated unit pre-trends?
├─ YES → Diff-in-Diff (use that donor)
└─ NO single fit → Synthetic Control (blend donors)
└─ NO control group at all → Interrupted Time Series (ITS) family
► Example: a website releases a website change with a 100% rollout, no control group, no untreated region → bottom-right: Interrupted Time Series family. That's the only viable path.

WITH A CONTROL GROUP

Ridge: These methods are powerful — but they need something you don't always have: an untreated comparison group observed at the same time. See the sub-chapters below for how each method works and how they differ.

DIFFERENCE-IN-DIFFERENCES (DID)

Effect = (Treatedafter − Treatedbefore) − (Controlafter − Controlbefore)
  • Requires: One treated group + one control group, both observed before and after
  • Key assumption:
    Parallel trendsBoth groups were on the same trajectory before the intervention. If the control group was already growing faster or slower, DiD gives a biased result. Always plot pre-period trends for both groups to check.
    — both groups would have moved identically without the intervention
  • Classic example: New tax policy in State A, neighboring State B as control
  • 100% rollout case: ✘ NOT APPLICABLE — no untreated customer group exists

SYNTHETIC CONTROL METHOD (SCM)

► Worked example: London TV + Synthetic Control vs DiD — sub-chapter SYNTHETIC CONTROL VS DID in the side nav below.
  • Requires: Multiple untreated donor units (e.g. 5 regions where the campaign didn't run)
  • How it works: Learn weights so the donor mix tracks the treated region before the campaign. After launch, compare actual treated region vs. the mix — the gap is the estimated effect.
  • Classic example: Ad campaign in London; Manchester, Bristol, Leeds, Edinburgh, Cardiff as donors (GeoLift)
  • 100% rollout case: ✘ NOT APPLICABLE — all regions received the change simultaneously
► Both methods fail for the 100% rollout case because the business rolled out to everyone. No untreated group exists at any level — no customer segment, no branch, no geography was left unchanged.
► For more detail on DiD vs A/B tests and DiD vs Synthetic Control, use the sub-chapters in the side nav (just below this slide).
SUB-CHAPTER · WITH A CONTROL GROUP

A/B TEST VS DID

Both compare a treated group to a control group, before and after. So what's the difference?

Randomized Controlled Trial (A/B Test)Difference-in-Differences (DiD)
Control group You create it by randomly assigning users to treatment and control before the change goes live You find an existing one — a region, a customer segment, or a product line that was not treated
Why it works Randomization guarantees groups are identical in expectation — no confounders, known or unknown. This is the gold standard. Assumes both groups would have followed the same trajectory without the intervention (parallel trends assumption). This cannot be verified — only made plausible.
Main risk Low — the main risk is insufficient sample size, not bias If the groups were already diverging before the intervention, the estimate is biased. Pre-period trend plots are essential.
Example Feature flag: 50% of app users see the new checkout flow; 50% see the old one. Both groups are observed simultaneously. New tax policy in one state; neighboring state as control. The neighboring state was never randomized — it just happened not to be treated.
► In one sentence: an A/B test creates its control group through randomization. DiD borrows an existing one and relies on the parallel-trends assumption holding true.
SUB-CHAPTER · WITH A CONTROL GROUP

SYNTHETIC CONTROL VS DID

A national retailer runs TV ads only in London for 2 weeks. How much extra revenue did London make because of TV?

WHY NOT MANCHESTER ALONE? (DID PATH)

Manchester didn't get TV — so it looks like a DiD control. But Manchester's daily revenue shape doesn't match London pre-TV (London flat, Manchester trending up). DiD with one bad twin → biased lift.

BUILD SYNTHETIC LONDON (SCM PATH)

Donor cities (no TV): Manchester, Bristol, Leeds, Edinburgh, Cardiff. Software picks weights so the mix tracks London before TV:

Synthetic London (day t) = 0.35 × Manchester(t) + 0.25 × Bristol(t) + 0.20 × Leeds(t) + 0.15 × Edinburgh(t) + 0.05 × Cardiff(t)
(illustrative weights — not chosen by hand)

SUB-CHAPTER · WITH A CONTROL GROUP

DID OR SCM?

FAQ

"WHO PICKS 35% MANCHESTER, 25% BRISTOL…?"

You don't. A Synthetic Control algorithm does — using only pre-campaign daily revenue for London and each donor city.

  • Input: Time series you already have (GA4, sales by region, etc.) — same dates, same metric.
  • Goal: Find weights so 0.35×Manchester + 0.25×Bristol + … hugs London's line before TV as closely as possible.
  • Rules: Weights are ≥ 0 and usually sum to 100% (a convex blend, not magic numbers).
  • Tools: Meta GeoLift, R Synth, Python — you run the fit; you don't spreadsheet the weights.
► Hard part isn't guessing weights — it's having good donors (untreated regions) and enough pre-period history so the blend can match London. If donors don't resemble London pre-TV, no method saves you.

"IF MANCHESTER ALREADY BEHAVES LIKE LONDON — DO I NEED SCM?"

Usually no — use Difference-in-Differences (DiD) with Manchester as the one control.

SituationUse
Plot pre-TV: Manchester and London move in parallel DiD — Manchester is your control; simpler, easier to explain
No single city tracks London pre-TV, but a mix could Synthetic Control — algorithm finds the mix
You average Bristol + Leeds + Manchester equally "because more controls" Not SCM — naive average ≠ optimal pre-fit; can bias DiD too
Concrete: Manchester ≈ London pre-TV

London gets TV; Manchester doesn't. For 90 days before TV, both rise ~2% per week. DiD subtracts Manchester's before→after change from London's — the shared trend cancels out. You do not need to blend five cities unless Manchester alone fails the parallel-trends check on the chart.

Concrete: Manchester is not a good twin alone

London flat pre-TV; Manchester trending up. DiD with only Manchester attributes Manchester's growth to "what London would have done" — overstates TV lift. Synthetic Control searches weights on Bristol, Leeds, Edinburgh… until the blend is flat like London. That's when SCM earns its complexity.

► More donor cities ≠ automatically Synthetic Control. More donors = more options for SCM if you need a blend. One good parallel donor = stay with DiD.

SHOULD I PICK ONE OR RUN BOTH?

They answer different questions — running both is not redundant, but the value depends on the intervention:

Intervention typePrimary methodAdd the other?
Temporary / bounded (promo, campaign) BSTS → cumulative Σα(t) Regression ITS β₃ ≈ 0 and noisy — adds little. Skip or note it's expected flat.
Permanent / structural (UX redesign, pricing change) Regression ITS → β₂ + β₃ Running BSTS adds convergent validity. But BSTS cumulative keeps growing indefinitely — interpret as "total so far as of day N", not a final number.
Unsure whether temporary or permanent Run both Plot BSTS daily α(t): if it stabilises → permanent (regression interpretation); if it decays to zero → temporary (BSTS cumulative is the metric).
► When you run both, don't average the numbers — they measure different things with different time horizons. Convergence means they agree on direction, not that the same number comes out.

EFFECT INTENSITY OVER TIME — WHICH FLAVOR?

If you want how the lift evolves day by day (spike then fade, ramp-up, etc.):

What you wantITS flavor
Level jump at T₀ + permanent change in growth rate Segmented ITS regression → β₂ + β₃. Best for permanent interventions.
Daily effect each post-day + cumulative total BSTS / CausalImpact → pointwise α(t) each day + Σα(t). Best for bounded campaigns.
Flexible curved path after intervention (splines) CausalPy ITS — Bayesian regression with spline terms.
Effect right at the cutoff only (local) RDiT — local polynomial, not a full post-campaign curve.
Concrete example — same promo, both paradigms

Regression ITS: Revenue jumps +£50k on day 1 of the promo (β₂ = level change), then grows an extra +£5k per day (β₃ = slope change). You get two numbers — not a smooth daily curve.

BSTS: Day 1 lift +£80k, day 2 +£60k, day 3 +£40k… plus a cumulative band. The model draws a full counterfactual line through the post-period so you see the shape of the effect, not just one break parameter.

SUB-CHAPTER · INTERRUPTED TIME SERIES

ITS REGRESSION VARIANTS

BEYOND THE SIMPLE MODEL

The "simple" segmented regression Y = β₀ + β₁t + β₂D + β₃(t−T₀)D is the baseline of the regression ITS paradigm. It assumes linear trend, no autocorrelation, and one breakpoint. Real-world data rarely cooperates — these variants address that.

VariantWhat it adds to the simple modelUse when
Autoregressive ITS
ARIMA-ITS		 Adds lagged Y terms: Y(t−1), Y(t−2)… alongside the D/slope terms Almost always — business metrics are autocorrelated (today's revenue predicts tomorrow's). Ignoring this inflates false positive rates because standard errors are too narrow.
Controlled ITS
ITS + comparison series		 Models the gap between the treated series and a concurrent non-treated comparison series — subtracts shared trends before fitting the break Strongest non-experimental ITS design (hybrid of ITS + DiD). Use when a comparable unaffected series exists (e.g. a product category not in the promo, or an untreated region).
Nonlinear trend ITS Replaces β₁t with polynomial or natural spline terms Pre-period trend visibly curves (common in growing e-commerce series). A straight line would misproject the counterfactual.
Multiple breakpoint ITS Adds a second (or third) D/slope-change pair — one for each intervention on the same series Sequential changes: site launch → redesign → feature drop. Each gets its own β₂/β₃.
Hierarchical ITS
Panel / multi-level		 Many units (stores, regions, product categories) each with their own β₂/β₃, sharing a common prior (Bayesian) or random effects (frequentist) Panel data — many units observed over time. Shares strength across units so small-sample units get better estimates.
► Where does BSTS fit? BSTS handles autocorrelation and nonlinear trend automatically via its state-space components. That is a practical reason it often outperforms plain regression ITS on messy daily data — you don't need to bolt on ARIMA or spline extensions by hand. But regression ITS is more transparent: you see β₂ and β₃ directly. Choose based on what you need: interpretability (regression) vs flexibility (BSTS).
CausalPy — where does it fit?

CausalPy implements the regression ITS formula (Y = β₀ + β₁t + β₂D + β₃(t−T₀)D) but estimates it with a Bayesian engine (PyMC). You get β₂ and β₃ as full posterior distributions (credible intervals) instead of point estimates + frequentist CIs — same paradigm as regression, richer uncertainty. It also supports spline terms for nonlinear trends.

SUB-CHAPTER · INTERRUPTED TIME SERIES

WHAT "FPR" MEANS

GENERIC · ANY BSTS / CAUSAL IMPACT STUDY

FALSE POSITIVE RATE (FPR)

False Positive Rate (FPR) = how often a method cries "significant!" when nothing actually happened.

The placebo thought experiment (no real campaign)

  1. Take your real pre-launch history (no UX change yet, or pick a period you know is quiet).
  2. Pretend the intervention started on a random fake date in that history.
  3. Run the same Causal Impact / BSTS pipeline you would run for real.
  4. Repeat 100× with different fake dates.
► If ~40 of 100 fake runs get p < 0.05, the method's p-values are too optimistic — about 40% false positive rate. Published benchmarks for BSTS-class tools land around 35–55% (generic literature + validation suites, not tied to one retailer).

WHAT WE ARE WARNING YOU ABOUT

  • Not: "Your result is wrong."
  • Not: "Shop x / bank y / retail z is special."
  • Yes: "The default p-value from the package is unreliable on its own."
  • Yes: "You need a second check — usually a permutation test on the real launch date."

Permutation on the real date (what to do instead)

Same shuffle idea, but compare: is the effect at the true launch date larger than effects at fake dates? If yes → more believable. Report permutation p, not only model p.

Why mention a retail case in the other deck then?

Only as a worked example where model p=0.02 but permutation p=0.47 on a bad specification — it illustrates the generic trap (over-long pre-period), not a rule that applies to one brand only.

SUB-CHAPTER · INTERRUPTED TIME SERIES

BANK CASE: FULL WALKTHROUGH

100% ROLLOUT · NO CONTROL GROUP

THE SCENARIO

A bank redesigns its online banking UX and rolls it out to all customers on the same day. No A/B test, no regional holdout. Goal: did the new UX increase engagement / product adoption?

WHY DID AND SCM ARE RULED OUT

  • DiD — no untreated customer group for parallel-trends comparison
  • SCM — no untreated branches or regions to use as donor units

RECOMMENDED: BSTS + RDIT CROSS-VALIDATION

Step 1 — Data requirements

  • Daily metric: logins, sessions, product applications, transaction volume
  • Pre-period: at least 3× the expected observation window (e.g. observe for 30 days → 90+ days of pre-data)
  • Exogenous covariates: market index, competitor app downloads, interest rate announcements, weather, day-of-week / holiday flags

Step 2 — Pre-period selection

  • Choose a homogeneous window — same seasonal regime as the post-launch period
  • Mask or exclude known disruptions (system outages, regulatory changes, prior redesigns)
  • A clean 60-day pre-period beats a noisy 400-day one

Step 3 — Covariate safety audit

  • Compare each covariate's last-7-days-pre mean vs treatment-period mean
  • If a covariate changed >10%, it may be absorbing the effect → flag as CAUTION
  • Exclude any metric downstream of the UX change (e.g. in-app clicks that the new design directly changed)

THE UX CHANGE NEVER "ENDS" — SENSIBLE ANALYSIS

The new UI stays live forever. Methods still need a defined evaluation window — you are measuring "impact as of day N", not waiting for an off-switch.

Reporting choiceWhat it means
Primary window (pre-register) e.g. first 30 days after launch — "early adoption effect"
Secondary window days 31–90 — "stabilized effect" (novelty may fade)
Monitoring read extend post-period to latest data each month; same model, new end date — label as updated estimate, not a new experiment
  • BSTS: post-period = launch date → end of chosen window. Counterfactual projects through that window only. Wider bands further from launch are normal.
  • Do not imply the effect "turns off" when the window ends — say: "estimated lift over the first 30 days post-launch."
  • Stakeholder line: "As of 90 days after go-live, engagement is X% above the modelled without-redesign path (95% interval …), with permutation p=…"
  • If the lift is a permanent level shift, segmented ITS / BSTS often show a step-up that persists — that matches an open-ended UX change well.
► Sensible default for the bank: BSTS + permutation on a pre-registered 30-day and 90-day read, refreshed monthly for monitoring, always stating the evaluation end date.
SUB-CHAPTER · INTERRUPTED TIME SERIES

BANK CASE: OUTPUT & REPORTING

WHAT THE OUTPUT LOOKS LIKE

Counterfactual chart — a time-series plot showing: (1) observed metric (solid line), (2) predicted counterfactual (dashed line), (3) credible interval band around the counterfactual. The gap between observed and counterfactual = estimated causal effect.
Cumulative effect — total incremental units (logins, applications, transactions) over the observation window. Comes with a 95% credible interval: "the UX change generated between X and Y additional product applications."
Probability of positive effect — Bayesian posterior probability that the true effect is >0. More intuitive for stakeholders than a p-value. "There is a 96% probability that the redesign had a positive effect on engagement."

WHAT TO REPORT TO STAKEHOLDERS

  1. Effect size with uncertainty: "+14% engagement, 95% CI [+4%, +24%]"
  2. Multi-method agreement: "BSTS estimates +14%, RDiT estimates +11% — both positive, direction consistent"
  3. Permutation validation: "Permutation p=0.04 — the effect is larger than 96% of random placebo dates"
  4. Honest caveats: "This is quasi-experimental, not a randomized trial. The estimate depends on the assumption that pre-period patterns would have continued."
► Lead with "probability of positive effect" for non-technical audiences. Keep p-values and CIs for the technical appendix. Always include caveats — it builds trust.

PRACTICAL CHECKLIST

Hootsworth: Don't overthink it. Answer these four questions and the method picks itself.
QuestionIf YESIf NO
Can you randomize into treated / control? A/B Test (RCT) — always preferred Continue ↓
Do you have an untreated group (region, segment, store)? DiD or Synthetic Control Continue ↓
Is the campaign ≤ 2 weeks with a sharp start date? RDiT as lead + BSTS as cross-check BSTS as lead + RDiT as cross-check
Do you have ≥ 3× campaign length of clean pre-data? Proceed with chosen ITS method Underpowered — report MDE, not significance

ALWAYS, REGARDLESS OF METHOD

  • Run a covariate safety audit before including any covariate
  • Validate with permutation tests — model p-values alone are unreliable
  • Use at least two methods for cross-validation
  • Report uncertainty honestly: credible intervals, not just point estimates
  • Consider a specification curve: test all analytical choices, show the distribution

  • Causal inference answers "did X cause Y?" — not just "did X and Y move together?" The missing counterfactual ("what would have happened without the intervention?") is the core challenge.
  • Causal effect = observed minus counterfactual. Every method is a different way to build the counterfactual.
