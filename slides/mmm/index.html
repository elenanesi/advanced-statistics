MMM FOR MEDIA PLANS

Ridge: Marketing Mix Modeling (MMM) is not a last-click report. It is a model of the shopping list — so you can rewrite next year’s media plan.

Work transfer · off-syllabus · cousin of OLS (ordinary least squares) with two extra “physics” terms.

The question it answers

“Where should next year’s media money go?”

They are not asking whether last month’s promo “worked”. They already have a media plan to write: channel totals, and which weeks those channels fly.

That is what MMM is for. You watch many weeks where the mix changed, reverse-engineer how sales respond to each channel, then propose a new mix.

You already have a tool for a different question. Causal impact (Bayesian structural time series — BSTS) asks: did this campaign add lift vs a counterfactual? MMM asks: if we change the whole mix next year, how does the KPI move?

Check yourself — one sentence

If your answer was “attribute conversions to touchpoints”, that is multi-touch attribution (MTA), not MMM. If it was “did the promo work?”, that is causal impact. MMM is the allocation engine.

Taste the soup, not the salt

You never taste salt alone. You taste soup every week. Over a hundred weeks you change how much salt, pepper, and stock you put in — and you reverse-engineer the recipe. Then you write next year’s shopping list.

Baseline (plain English: sales you would still get with £0 media — brand, distribution, season, habit) is the stock already in the pot. Media is the seasoning. MMM estimates how much each seasoning moved the taste.

Four tools that get confused

Same boardroom sentence — “what did media do?” — four different objects. The fork is the question, not the dashboard.

Causal impact / BSTS

Did this campaign add lift vs a no-campaign world? One intervention, one series. Post-mortem. You already use this.

MTA / last-click

Which touchpoint sat on the path of people who converted? User-level credit. Digital-only. Not incrementality of spend.

A/B or geo-lift

Gold-standard incrementality for one tactic. Expensive. Too slow to rewrite a full annual plan every quarter.

MMM

If we change the mix of spend across channels and weeks, how does the KPI move? Built to output a new plan.

Lookalike trap: platform ROAS (return on ad spend) from Meta or Google is closer to MTA than to MMM. It is not “what would sales have been if we had not spent”.

What a media plan actually is

A media plan is a calendar of spend — not a ranking of channels. Rows are channels. Columns are weeks or months. Each cell is “how much we buy here”.

Split last year’s sales

First output is a decomposition: how much of sales was baseline vs each channel. Tiny retailer, made-up but consistent numbers we will reuse.

Average ROI = incremental sales / spend. Useful as a rear-view mirror. It is the wrong number for rewriting the plan — next two slides say why.

The regression skeleton (after the picture)
$Sales_t = Baseline_t + \sum_c f_c(\tilde{x}_{c,t}) + Controls_t + error_t$

$f_c$ is that channel’s saturation curve. $\tilde{x}$ is adstocked spend. Baseline swallows intercept + season + trend. Same family as OLS; the two extra physics terms are why this is not “regress sales on spend”.

Adstock — the echo

Why this exists: a TV spot on Monday still sells on Thursday. If you credit only the spend week, you under-count brand channels and over-count always-on digital.

Picture: food colouring in a glass (TV — the water stays tinted). An ice cube (Search — gone when it melts, this week).

Media-plan consequence: decay speed writes the calendar. Slow echo → bursts can be efficient (spend, then ride the tint). Fast echo → always-on, or you are dark.
SUB-CHAPTER

Geometric adstock

Turn this week’s spend $x_t$ into “effective media” $\tilde{x}_t$ by keeping a fraction $\lambda$ of last week’s echo.

$\tilde{x}_t = x_t + \lambda\,\tilde{x}_{t-1}$

$\lambda$ near 0.5–0.8: TV, video, OOH (out-of-home). $\lambda$ near 0–0.2: search, shopping. The model estimates $\lambda$ per channel from the data (often with a prior in Bayesian MMM).

Then sales respond to $\tilde{x}$, not to raw $x$. That is how a Monday spot is allowed to explain Thursday’s sales.

Why geometric, not “lag 1, lag 2, lag 3…” as separate regressors?

Free lags eat degrees of freedom and collinear with each other. One $\lambda$ is a one-knob echo. Same job as an exponentially weighted moving average of spend.

Saturation — leftover pizza

Why this exists: the first £ of search is hungry customers you were not already reaching. The 8-millionth £ of TV is the same people, again. Returns fall. That curve is the whole reason a plan can be wrong even when every channel has positive ROI.

The slope at the current point is the return on the next pound. Average ROI is the slope of the chord from the origin. Those two numbers tell different stories on purpose.

SUB-CHAPTER

The Hill curve

A common saturation shape (Hill / Michaelis–Menten family): extra spend still helps, but less and less, and it never goes negative.

$f(x)=\beta\dfrac{x^{\alpha}}{x^{\alpha}+\gamma^{\alpha}}$
  • $\beta$ — ceiling: max sales this channel can ever add.
  • $\gamma$ — the spend level at which you are halfway to the ceiling (the “half-saturation” knob).
  • $\alpha$ — how sharp the elbow is. $\alpha=1$ is a smooth bend; large $\alpha$ is more of a cliff then a plateau.

You do not need to fit this by hand. You need to read it: if the plan sits past $\gamma$, the next pound is cheap-looking in average ROI and expensive in slope.

Average ROI vs the next pound

Two lookalikes. Mixing them up is how good channels get cut and saturated channels get fed.

Average ROI

Total incremental sales / total spend. Rear-view. “What did this channel return this year?”

Marginal ROI (mROI)

Slope of the response curve at current spend. Forward-looking. “What does the next pound return?”

ChannelSpendAvg ROImROI nowRead
TV£8.0M1.500.60Saturated. Next £ returns 60p of sales.
Search£2.0M3.002.10Still steep. Next £ returns £2.10.
Social£1.5M1.601.40Healthy. Not the star, not done.
The trap: a planner who sorts by average ROI pours more into Search and also protects TV because “1.5 is fine”. The plan question is mROI vs a hurdle — and the hurdle should use contribution margin, not revenue. If margin is 50%, you need mROI ≥ 2 just to break even on the next pound.

Rewrite the plan (numbers)

Same £11.5M budget. Move £1.0M from TV → Search. Use the current slopes as a local guess (honest caveat below).

TV incrementalSearch incrementalTotal sales
Now£12.00M£6.00M£40.00M
After £1M shift £11.40M (−0.60) £8.10M (+2.10) £41.50M

Same money, +£1.5M sales — if the slopes stay put. They do not. After the shift, TV’s mROI rises (less pizza) and Search’s mROI falls (more pizza). A real optimizer walks along the curves until mROIs are equal (or hit a constraint), not one £1M leap.

This is the media-plan move: you are not “picking a winner”. You are moving money down the leftover-pizza channel and up the still-hungry one, until the next pound is worth the same everywhere you are allowed to spend.

From curves to a calendar

The optimizer’s real output is not a bar chart of ROI. It is a new heatmap: channel × week, under constraints the planner already has (min TV for brand, contracts, production lead time, cannot 10× search in a week without CPCs exploding).

What MMM cannot plan

Grain is usually market × week × channel (sometimes tactic). Everything finer than that is a different tool.

MMM can rewriteStill a human / another model
Channel budgets (year / quarter)Creative, endline, offer
Burst vs always-on (if weekly)Audience, keyword, placement inside a platform
Scenarios: “same money, more search”Dayparting, bidding, frequency caps
A forecast of the KPI under a planA new channel with no history (needs a test first)
If someone hands you an MMM and asks which Meta lookalike to buy, they have the wrong instrument. That is in-platform incrementality or a test, not mix modeling.

Also not a substitute for causal impact on a single national promo. Different question, different output (cumulative lift vs a counterfactual, not a 12-month heatmap).

Traps

  • Sorting the plan by average ROI. You already saw this. mROI + constraints rewrite the calendar.
  • No spend variation. If search is a flat always-on line, the model has nothing to learn. It will invent a plausible coefficient anyway.
  • Lockstep channels. TV and OOH that only ever fly together in Q4 share one effect. The split is a story, not a measurement. (Next sub-chapter.)
  • Last-click as ground truth. If you “validate” MMM against Google last-click, you are forcing it to reproduce a biased report.
  • Ignoring controls. Price, promo, distribution, competitors, weather, season. Leave them out and media eats their credit (or vice versa).
  • Acting on one refresh. Plans are sticky. Use MMM for the next planning cycle and for scenario tables, not for Tuesday’s bid change.
SUB-CHAPTER

Collinearity

If two rows of the media plan are nearly the same shape, MMM cannot taste them apart. It sees one seasoning called “Q4 brand pile-up”.

Fixes that are actually plan decisions: stagger flights, hold one channel dark in a region, or run a geo-test on one of them. A prettier Bayesian prior does not create information the calendar never provided.

SUB-CHAPTER

Calibrate with tests

MMM is observational. Experiments pin a channel down. That is how this sits next to work you already do.

  • Geo-lift / causal impact on one channel → a number you trust for that tactic. Use it as a prior or a constraint so MMM cannot wander off.
  • Holdouts in the media plan (a dark week, a dark region) are not waste. They are identification. Planners hate them; the model needs them.
  • New channel (TikTok, retail media): no history → do not ask MMM to invent a response curve. Test first, then add a row to the plan and the model.
Division of labour: tests tell you the truth in one cell. MMM copies that truth across the rest of the calendar and the other cells you cannot test every quarter.

Quiz

1. Search avg ROI = 3.0, TV = 1.5. A planner zeros TV. What did they mix up?

Answer 1

They sorted by average ROI and ignored saturation, constraints, and echo. TV’s mROI was 0.60 — cut some, not necessarily all. Brand, contracts, and slow adstock can still justify a smaller burst pattern. Zeroing a channel is a plan decision with extra costs MMM’s slope does not price.

2. Can MMM tell you which TikTok creative to run next month?

Answer 2

No. Wrong grain. Creative is inside a cell of the plan. MMM rewrites cells (channel × week), not the ad inside the cell. Use a test or in-platform incrementality.

3. TV and OOH only ever fly together in Q4. The model says OOH ROI is 4.0 and TV is 0.9. Trust it?

Answer 3

No. Collinearity: one bump, two labels. The split is unidentified. Stagger, geo-holdout, or report them as a combined brand block until the plan creates independent variation.

Quest complete

Ridge: If someone shows you average ROI and calls it a plan, ask for the response curve and the heatmap.
  • MMM answers “how should the mix change?”, not “did this promo work?” and not “who got last click?”
  • A media plan is a calendar. Adstock writes burst vs always-on; saturation writes where the next pound goes (mROI, not average ROI).
  • The usable output is a new channel×week plan under constraints — plus honesty about what the calendar never identified.
