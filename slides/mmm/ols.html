OLS — THE RULER

Ridge: Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) is a ruler through a cloud of points. The A/B t-test button is the same ruler, with X set to 0 or 1.

Sibling of the MMM media-plan deck in this folder. Not Task 5 (degree-10 polynomial + ridge) — that is OLS as a curve-fitting weapon. This is OLS as a measurement instrument.

You already run this

“What’s the lift?”

A planner or PM wants the difference in a KPI between variant B and variant A. The platform prints a t-test. That t-test is OLS: outcome on a 0/1 flag.

Your belief is right — and stronger than “OLS is also used”. For a simple two-cell experiment, difference-in-means, two-sample t-test, and OLS of $Y$ on treatment are the same number.

OLS did not sneak into A/B later as a fancy extra. The default analysis always was a linear model with one dummy (a 0/1 column).

Check yourself before the pictures

What does OLS minimise? The sum of squared vertical leftovers (residuals), not “correlation”, not “closest line in Euclidean distance to each point” (that would be orthogonal regression).

Are t-test and OLS different methods? Different buttons. Same estimator, when X is a treatment flag and you use the equal-variance t on the slope.

Square leftovers

Why this exists: you want one summary of “how Y moves when X moves” that is unique and doesn’t let one huge leftover hide by going negative. Squaring does both: positives, and a single minimum.

Picture: a rigid ruler, and a spring from each point straight down (or up) onto the ruler. Springs store energy as stretch². OLS is where the ruler comes to rest.

Vertical leftovers, not the shortest distance to the line. That choice is the “ordinary” in OLS — we treat X as given and Y as the thing we missed.

Three names, not three tools

These get taught as a zoo. For a randomised two-cell test they collapse.

Lookalike: “running OLS” vs “running a t-test” sounds like a methodology choice. It is a UI choice. The estimator did not change.

Ridge (Task 5) is a different estimator: OLS plus a spring that shrinks coefficients. Not this deck.

Five points, one line

Tiny numbers, so the recipe is visible. $x = 1,2,3,4,5$, $y = 3,5,4,8,7$.

MeanSlope $\hat\beta_1$Intercept $\hat\beta_0$
These five points$\bar x=3$, $\bar y=5.4$1.12.1

The fitted ruler: $\hat y = 2.1 + 1.1 x$. At $x=4$, the line says 6.5; the point is 8; leftover $= +1.5$. OLS picked 1.1 because any other slope makes the sum of leftover² bigger.

In software this is LinearRegression / statsmodels.OLS / numpy.linalg.lstsq. Same object.

SUB-CHAPTER

Normal equations

Once you accept “minimise leftover²”, the rest is calculus. Differentiate, set to zero, get a linear system.

$\hat\beta = (X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}y$

$X$ is the table of predictors plus a column of 1s (the intercept). For the five points, two columns: 1s and $x$. The inverse exists if the columns are not copies of each other (no perfect collinearity).

You do not need to invert this by hand. You do need to know when it cannot: two identical predictors, or more columns than rows (Task 5’s polynomial world).

Two means ARE OLS

Same ruler. X is no longer “spend”. X is which cell were you in.

$Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 T_i + \varepsilon_i \quad T_i \in \{0,1\}$

$\hat\beta_0 = \bar Y_{\text{control}}$ — the control mean. $\hat\beta_1 = \bar Y_{\text{variant}} - \bar Y_{\text{control}}$ — the lift. There is no extra sauce. The line has to pass through both means because those are the only two X-values.

Why write an A/B as a regression

If it is the same number, why not just subtract two means? Because the OLS form is the socket everything else plugs into.

  • Covariates — add last week’s revenue, device, geo. Treatment coefficient stays the lift; leftover noise drops. (Next slide.)
  • More than two cells — three creatives = two dummies. That is ANOVA, which is OLS.
  • Interactions — “does it work more on mobile?” is $T \times \text{mobile}$.
  • Standard errors that match the design — heteroskedasticity-robust, or clustered if you randomised cookies but analyse users (or the reverse).
Writing OLS does not make an observational comparison into an experiment. The randomisation did the causal work. OLS is the calculator.

Add a covariate

People who already spend a lot still spend a lot. If you ignore that, the leftover cloud is fat and the t-stat is shy. Add the pre-period metric as a second X.

$Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 T_i + \beta_2 \text{pre}_i + \varepsilon_i$

Still OLS. $\hat\beta_1$ is now the lift at the same pre-period. That is ANCOVA (analysis of covariance). CUPED is this idea with a specific recipe for $\beta_2$.

SUB-CHAPTER

CUPED is OLS in a trench coat

Controlled-experiment using pre-experiment data (CUPED): take the leftover of $Y$ after you subtract the part predicted by a pre-metric, then compare leftover means. That leftover is less noisy, so the same lift is easier to see.

If you fit $Y \sim T + \text{pre}$ with OLS and read the $T$ coefficient, you are in the same family. The “trench coat” is: they often estimate the pre-slope on a pooled sample (or pre-period only) so the adjustment is not fishing inside the test window.

Use it when the pre-metric actually predicts the KPI (last week’s revenue → this week’s revenue: yes. Last week’s revenue → a brand survey: usually no). Garbage covariate, no variance win.

Randomisation does the causal work

OLS always gives a slope. The slope is a causal lift only if $T$ is as-if random (or you have another identification strategy).

A/B — OLS is safe to read as lift

You assigned $T$ with a coin. High-spenders are in both cells. $\hat\beta_1$ is incrementality of the variant.

Observational — OLS is a description

People who saw more ads converted more. Maybe ads work; maybe they were already going to buy. Same calculator, different claim.

This is the fork with MMM and with last-click. MMM is closer to the right-hand box (observational mix) plus extra physics. Causal impact is “no random T, but a time break”. A/B is the left-hand box.

Never report an observational OLS coefficient with the same sentence you use for an A/B lift. The estimator did not change. The design did.

MMM is OLS plus physics

Same family, different $X$. In the media-plan deck, raw weekly spend is not the column you dump in. You first turn spend into effective media (adstock) and bend it (saturation), then OLS-or-Bayesian-OLS explains sales.

If someone “runs OLS of sales on TV spend” and calls it MMM, they assumed every extra pound returns the same, with no echo. That is the leftover-pizza mistake from the other deck, in calculator form.

Traps

  • “I used a t-test, not OLS.” For two cells, you used OLS. You just didn’t type sm.OLS.
  • OLS on a conversion 0/1 as if it were revenue. That is the linear probability model (next sub). It can work for A/B lift (the coefficient is still a difference in rates). It is a bad probability engine (predictions outside 0–1, squeezed SEs).
  • Covariates chosen after seeing the lift. That’s p-hacking. Pre-register the pre-metric, or use only pre-period data to pick it.
  • Wrong grain of randomisation. Randomised households, analysed sessions without clustering → over-confident p-values.
  • Reading OLS on logged Y as “+% lift” without the smearing/approximation caveats. A/B on log is not the same as A/B on £.
SUB-CHAPTER

Binary Y: LPM vs logistic

KPI is convert / not. Two common calculators:

OLS / linear probability model

$\hat\beta_1$ = difference in conversion rates. Same as a t-test on the 0/1s. Fine as an A/B lift. Ugly as a predicted probability.

Logistic regression

Models log-odds. Better probability engine. The coefficient is not a percentage-point lift; you have to translate.

For a randomised A/B where you need “+1.2 percentage points”, OLS on the 0/1 is the honest headline. Logistic is for when you need $P(Y{=}1 \mid x)$ that stays in (0,1).

Quiz

1. Control mean £10.5, variant mean £13.5. You fit $Y \sim T$. What are $\hat\beta_0$ and $\hat\beta_1$?

Answer 1

$\hat\beta_0 = 10.5$ (control mean). $\hat\beta_1 = 3$ (the lift). If you said “the slope is something else because regression is more sophisticated”, that is the lookalike trap.

2. You add last week’s revenue as a covariate. The treatment coefficient moves from 3.0 to 2.8 and the standard error halves. Did the test “stop being an A/B”?

Answer 2

No. Still randomised. You used a tighter ruler. 2.8 is the lift at the same pre-period. The SE drop is the point of CUPED/ANCOVA. Don’t pick the covariate after peeking at which one maximises the lift.

3. Sales ~ TV spend, no transforms, call it MMM. What’s wrong?

Answer 3

Plain OLS assumes constant returns and no echo. MMM’s extra physics (adstock + saturation) are exactly what make a media plan from the slope. You ran a description of correlation, not a mix model.

Quest complete

Ridge: If you know “fit a line, minimise leftover²”, you know OLS. The A/B move is: the line’s X is the coin flip.
  • OLS = unique ruler that minimises leftover². The linear model is the story; OLS is the picker.
  • Simple A/B lift = OLS of $Y$ on a 0/1. Same number as a t-test. Regression form is for covariates, variants, SEs.
  • Randomisation makes $\hat\beta_1$ causal. MMM uses the same calculator on transformed spend, without a coin flip.
