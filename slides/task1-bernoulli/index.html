Why Do We Need Formalism?

Penny: "For" and "against" are words. You can't add words. You can't compute an average of words. The whole point of probability theory is to turn real-world situations into numbers — then do math on the numbers.

Here's the problem we're solving:

Now we can compute: average, variance, probability of events — because we're working with 1s and 0s, not words.

Every piece of formalism in this task (Ω, F, P, measurability...) is just a precise way to describe this translation. The math isn't the point — the translation is. The math just makes the translation rigorous so everyone agrees on what you mean.

The Probability Space

A probability space (Ω, F, P) is the formal way to say: "here's my experiment, here's what I'm allowed to ask about it, and here's how likely each thing is."

Plain-English version: Ω is your menu of possible outcomes. F is the list of questions you're allowed to ask ("what's the probability of X?"). P is the answer machine that assigns a number between 0 and 1 to each question. You need all three together to do probability correctly.

Random Variable: The Label Gun

Penny: Think of a label gun at a supermarket. It doesn't change the product — it just sticks a price tag on it. A random variable is the same: it sticks a number tag on each outcome. "for" gets tag 1. "against" gets tag 0. That's literally all X(·) does.

X maps every outcome in Ω to a number in R. That's the whole definition of "random variable."

X("for") = 1 ← label assigned to "for" X("against") = 0 ← label assigned to "against" Shorthand: X ~ Bernoulli(0.58)

Why 1 and 0? Convention — but also convenient. 0 = "event didn't happen", 1 = "event happened". This lets us write E[X] = P(event happened), which is elegant.

But wait — why is X called "random" if 0 and 1 are fixed?

This trips everyone up. The answer: the function X(·) is completely deterministic. It always maps "for" → 1 and "against" → 0. That never changes.

The randomness is in the INPUT — which ballot enters the function. Before the vote is cast, you have no idea if ω will be "for" or "against". You don't know which label gun trigger will be pulled. That uncertainty is where X gets its randomness from.

Convention to know: capital X = the random variable (the rule + the uncertainty about what input arrives). Lowercase x = a specific realized value (what you actually observed after the vote: 0 or 1). Examiners use this distinction precisely.

Measurability — Plain English

The exam asks you to prove X is "F-measurable." This sounds scary. Here's what it actually means:

In plain English: X is measurable if — for any threshold you name — you can figure out which outcomes fall below it. "Tell me which outcomes give X ≤ 0.5." Measurability says you can always answer that question, and the answer is a legitimate event in F.

The threshold test — visually

For every possible threshold x, the set of outcomes "below" it is one of: ∅, {"against"}, Ω — all of which are in F. Measurability is automatic for indicator functions. You just need to state this, not re-derive it from scratch.

SUB-CHAPTER

Integrability — Why It's Trivial Here

A random variable X is integrable if its expected absolute value is finite: E[|X|] < ∞.

This sounds like it could be hard to check — but for bounded variables it's automatic.

Bernoulli — bounded, trivially integrable

Cauchy — unbounded tails, NOT integrable

The Cauchy distribution is the famous example where the mean doesn't exist — the expected absolute value is infinite. You can't compute an average. That's why integrability matters.

For your Bernoulli variable: X is always 0 or 1, so |X| is always ≤ 1. There's no tail, no infinity problem. E[|X|] ≤ 1 < ∞, so X is integrable. You state this in one sentence and move on.

Bernoulli Distribution

X ~ Bernoulli(p) models any single trial with two outcomes: success (1) with probability p, failure (0) with probability 1−p.

SUB-CHAPTER

PMF vs PDF — Don't Mix Them

Bernoulli is discrete — it only takes values 0 and 1. You use a PMF (Probability Mass Function).

PMF: probability AT specific points
P(X=0) = 0.42, P(X=1) = 0.58

PDF: probability is AREA under curve
P(X = any single value) = 0

Common mistake: calling P(X = 1) = 0.58 a "density." It's a probability mass. Bernoulli is discrete — you sum, not integrate. If you write "density" the examiner will notice.

Expectation — Center of Gravity

E[X] in one line: weighted average of all possible values — each value multiplied by its probability, summed up. It's the number the long-run average converges to after many trials. Not a prediction of one trial; a property of the distribution.
From the intro deck: die E[X] = 1·(1/6) + 2·(1/6) + … + 6·(1/6) = 3.5 — even though 3.5 is never rolled.
Penny: For Bernoulli, the only values are 0 and 1 — so the weighted average is just: 0 × P(against) + 1 × P(for). The seesaw below shows it geometrically.

The derivation

$$E[X] = \sum_k k \cdot P(X = k)$$
$= 0 \cdot P(X=0) + 1 \cdot P(X=1)$
$= 0 \cdot 0.42 \;+\; 1 \cdot 0.58$
$= \mathbf{0.58}$  ← balance point of the distribution
The "for" side (value=1, weight=0.58) pulls harder than the "against" side (value=0, weight=0.42). The balance point lands at 0.58 — closer to 1 than to 0. If both sides were equal (p=0.5), it would balance exactly in the middle.
SUB-CHAPTER

E[X] is NOT a Prediction

E[X] = 0.58 describes what happens on average over many trials. For one vote: the outcome is 0 or 1. Nothing in between.

Analogy: the average household has 2.4 children. You will never meet a family with exactly 2.4 children. But if you surveyed 10,000 families, the average would be close to 2.4. Expectation is a property of the model, not of any single observation.

This is the key nuance the exam asks you to address: "Can expectation be calculated?" Answer: yes, mathematically it is well-defined — AND a single vote produces a realization (0 or 1), never the expectation value itself.

The Formal Proof (Write This)

Now that you understand the intuition, here's the compact version to write in the notebook under "Model and assumptions" + "Derivation":

PROBABILITY SPACE Ω = { "for", "against" } F = { ∅, {"for"}, {"against"}, Ω } ← power set; trivially σ-algebra P({"for"}) = 0.58, P({"against"}) = 0.42, P(Ω) = 1 RANDOM VARIABLE X = 1_{vote="for"} : Ω → {0,1} ⊆ R X("for") = 1, X("against") = 0 MEASURABILITY (preimage check) x < 0: X^{-1}(-∞,x] = ∅ ∈ F ✓ 0 ≤ x < 1: X^{-1}(-∞,x] = {"against"} ∈ F ✓ x ≥ 1: X^{-1}(-∞,x] = Ω ∈ F ✓ → X is F-measurable. INTEGRABILITY |X(ω)| ≤ 1 for all ω → E[|X|] ≤ 1 < ∞ → X is P-integrable. EXPECTATION E[X] = 0·P(X=0) + 1·P(X=1) = 0·0.42 + 1·0.58 = 0.58
One-sentence nuance to add: "Note that E[X] = 0.58 is a property of the distribution; a single observed vote takes values in {0,1}, not {0.58}."

Common Pitfalls

1. Skipping branch resolution

Wrong: jumping to p = 0.58 without stating ξ1 = 0 applies.
Right: "Since ξ1 = 0, the Bernoulli branch applies. Meteorite sub-tasks are not performed."

2. E[X] = "most likely outcome"

Wrong: "E[X] = 0.58 means the vote will probably be 'for'." (That's the mode, not expectation.)
Right: E[X] = 0.58 is the long-run average. The most likely single outcome is also "for" (since p > 0.5) — but that's a separate statement.

3. Y-axis not starting at 0

Wrong: y-axis from 40% to 65% — makes a 16-point gap look huge.
Right: y-axis 0 to 100%. Honest representation of proportions.

4. Writing "density" for a discrete distribution

Wrong: "The density at X=1 is 0.58."
Right: "The probability mass at X=1 is P(X=1) = 0.58."

5. Skipping the spot check

Wrong: showing the chart without verifying P(for) + P(against) = 1.
Right: one print statement, five seconds, full marks for tool trust.

Quick-Fire Quiz

Q1

In plain English, what does "X is F-measurable" actually mean for this experiment?

Reveal answer

It means: for any number x you pick, you can always identify which outcomes give X ≤ x, and that set of outcomes is something F "knows about" (i.e., it's in F, so we can assign it a probability). For our indicator X ∈ {0,1}, the possible preimage sets are ∅, {"against"}, and Ω — all in F. So yes, X is measurable.

Q2

Someone observes 10 votes and gets: for, for, against, for, against, for, for, against, for, for. The fraction "for" is 7/10 = 0.70. Does this contradict E[X] = 0.58?

Reveal answer

No — not at all. E[X] = 0.58 is the long-run average, not a guarantee for any 10 samples. Getting 70% "for" in 10 trials is entirely consistent with p = 0.58. With small samples, variance is high. You'd need thousands of votes for the sample fraction to converge reliably to 0.58 (by the Law of Large Numbers).

Q3

Why does the seesaw balance at 0.58 and not at 0.5 (the midpoint between 0 and 1)?

Reveal answer

Because the weights are unequal: 0.58 on the "1" side and 0.42 on the "0" side. The balance point is pulled toward the heavier side. If p = 0.5 (equal weights), it would balance at 0.5. With p = 0.58, the "for" side is heavier, so the balance point is 0.58 — closer to 1 than to 0. Mathematically: E[X] = 0×0.42 + 1×0.58 = 0.58.
