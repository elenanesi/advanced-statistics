Modeling Time-to-Event

Real-world examples of time-to-event models:

  • Reliability engineering: How long until a machine fails?
  • Medicine: How long until a patient recovers (or relapses)?
  • Finance: How long until a loan defaults?
  • Your task: How long until the owl calls?
All of these are modeled with survival analysis. The core question is always: "At time y, what's the probability the event HASN'T happened yet?" That probability as a function of y is the survival function.

CDF vs Survival Function

There are two equivalent ways to describe how a distribution spreads over time. They're mirror images of each other.

$$\bar{F}_Y(y) = 1 - F_Y(y) \qquad \bar{F}_Y(y) + F_Y(y) = 1 \text{ always}$$ $$F_Y(y) = 1 - \bar{F}_Y(y)$$

Your exam gives you F̄_Y(y) directly. Everything you need — the PDF, probabilities, mean — can be derived from it.

Why give you the survival function instead of the CDF? In time-to-event problems, the survival function is more natural. "What fraction of owls are still unheard at time y?" is a more intuitive question than "What fraction have been heard by y?" — both are valid, just convention.

Mixture = Two Populations Blended

Hootsworth: Imagine two types of nights: "quiet nights" where I call early (~22 min average), and "slow nights" where I take longer (~27 min average). 80% of nights are slow, 20% are quick. If you sample a night at random, you get a mixture of both behaviors. That's what the two terms model.
$$\bar{F}(y) = \underbrace{0.79}_{\substack{\text{weight of}\\\text{slow component}}} \cdot e^{-4y^2} \;+\; \underbrace{0.20}_{\substack{\text{weight of}\\\text{fast component}}} \cdot e^{-6y^2}$$

The combined survival is a weighted average of two "pure" survival curves. When you observe a random night, 79% of the time you're drawing from the slower-decay distribution, 20% from the faster one.

From Survival Function to PDF

Hootsworth: Think of the survival curve like water draining from a tank. The current level is S(y). The rate at which water is leaving the tank right now is the density f(y). Fast drain = high density. The PDF is just "how quickly is S(y) dropping?"

The tank drains fastest when it's between ¼ and ½ full — that's where the PDF peaks (around 20-25 min).

The formula

$$f_Y(y) = -\frac{d}{dy}\,\bar{F}_Y(y)$$ The negative sign: $\bar{F}(y)$ is decreasing, so its derivative is negative. We negate it to get a positive density.

The logic: if S(y) drops by 0.10 between y=0.4 and y=0.5, that means 10% of owls are heard in that half-hour window — and the density there is high.

Deriving the PDF — Step by Step

Start with: $$\bar{F}(y) = 0.79\,e^{-4y^2} + 0.20\,e^{-6y^2}$$ Differentiate term by term using chain rule: $\tfrac{d}{dy}[e^{-4y^2}] = -8y\,e^{-4y^2}$ $\tfrac{d}{dy}[e^{-6y^2}] = -12y\,e^{-6y^2}$ So: $\tfrac{d}{dy}\bar{F}(y) = 0.79\cdot(-8y)\,e^{-4y^2} + 0.20\cdot(-12y)\,e^{-6y^2}$ Apply $f(y) = -\tfrac{d}{dy}\bar{F}(y)$: $$f_Y(y) = 0.79\cdot 8y\,e^{-4y^2} + 0.20\cdot 12y\,e^{-6y^2}$$ $$\boxed{f_Y(y) = 6.32\,y\,e^{-4y^2} + 2.40\,y\,e^{-6y^2}}$$

Sanity check — integrate to verify

$\displaystyle\int_0^\infty a\,y\,e^{-by^2}\,dy = \frac{a}{2b}$   [standard result] $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty 6.32\,y\,e^{-4y^2}\,dy = \frac{6.32}{8} = 0.79$  ✓ $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty 2.40\,y\,e^{-6y^2}\,dy = \frac{2.40}{12} = 0.20$ ✓ Total: $0.79 + 0.20 = 0.99$ ← confirms the 0.01 gap

The PDF is zero at y=0 (you can't hear an instantaneous sound), peaks around 21 min, then decays. This Rayleigh-like shape is typical for "shortest time to an event with a quadratic hazard."

Computing P(2 < Y < 4)

The exam asks for P(2 < Y < 4). The survival function gives us interval probabilities elegantly:

For any $a < b$: $$P(a < Y < b) = P(Y > a) - P(Y > b) = \bar{F}(a) - \bar{F}(b)$$

Why this works — visually

The calculation

$\bar{F}(2) = 0.79\,e^{-16} + 0.20\,e^{-24}$ $\approx 0.79 \times 1.13\times 10^{-7} + 0.20 \times 3.78\times 10^{-11}$ $\approx 8.89\times 10^{-8}$ (essentially zero) $\bar{F}(4) \approx 0$ (even smaller) $$P(2 < Y < 4) = \bar{F}(2) - \bar{F}(4) \approx 8.89\times 10^{-8} \approx 0$$
This is the correct answer — and an important one. The owl is almost certainly heard within the first hour (S(1) = 0.015). By 2 hours, the probability of still waiting is 0.0000001. P(2 < Y < 4) is not zero by mistake — it reflects that the distribution puts essentially all its mass in the first hour. State this explicitly in the notebook.

Unit Conversion: Hours → Minutes

The exam asks for a "minute-level histogram." Your survival function uses Y in hours. You need to convert to minutes and simulate/plot in those units.

Why f_T(t) = f_Y(t/60) / 60?

This is the change-of-variables formula. If T = 60Y, then Y = T/60, and the PDF transforms as:

$$f_T(t) = f_Y\!\left(\tfrac{t}{60}\right)\cdot\left|\frac{dY}{dT}\right| = \frac{f_Y(t/60)}{60}$$ The /60 is the Jacobian — it keeps the PDF integrating to 1. (Without the ÷60, the area under the curve would become 60, not 1.)

Why the Jacobian — Intuition

The /60 factor feels mysterious. Here's why it must be there.

The probability in the strip (the area) must be the same whether you measure in hours or minutes — it's the same physical event. The strip is 6× wider in minutes, so the height must be 6× shorter (÷60). The area stays the same. That's the Jacobian.

General rule: if T = c·Y, then f_T(t) = f_Y(t/c)/c. The /c compensates for the c× stretching of the axis.

Mean, Variance & Quartiles

For a continuous distribution, these are integrals — but your exam expects numerical computation via scipy or numpy. The formula is the same concept as Bernoulli, just with an integral instead of a sum.

$$E[Y] = \int_0^\infty y \cdot f_Y(y)\,dy \qquad \text{← continuous version of } \textstyle\sum_x x\cdot P(X=x)$$ $$\text{Var}(Y) = \int_0^\infty y^2 \cdot f_Y(y)\,dy \;-\; \bigl(E[Y]\bigr)^2$$ Median: solve $\bar{F}(m) = 0.495$   (= 0.99/2, accounting for the gap) Q1: solve $\bar{F}(q) = 0.99\times 0.75 = 0.7425$ Q3: solve $\bar{F}(q) = 0.99\times 0.25 = 0.2475$

Your numerical results

Mean slightly right of median — the right tail (rare slow nights) pulls the mean up. This is characteristic of right-skewed distributions.

The mean (25.35 min) is right of the median (23.93 min) because there's a long right tail — occasionally the owl takes much longer. A few slow nights pull the average up without moving the median (which only cares about the middle rank).

Common Pitfalls

1. Wrong sign when deriving PDF

Wrong: f(y) = d/dy F̄(y) → negative values.
Right: f(y) = −d/dy F̄(y). The survival function decreases, so its derivative is negative. You negate to get a positive PDF.

2. Not flagging the 0.01 gap

Wrong: proceeding without comment.
Right: explicitly state F̄(0) = 0.99 ≠ 1, discuss possible causes (rounding or point mass at 0), confirm that ∫f(y)dy = 0.99 (not 1), and proceed.

3. Ignoring what P(2 < Y < 4) ≈ 0 means

Wrong: reporting 8.89×10⁻⁸ without comment.
Right: state clearly that this is essentially zero, and explain why: the distribution puts almost all probability in [0, 1.5h], so the interval [2h, 4h] is in the extreme tail.

4. Forgetting the /60 Jacobian

Wrong: f_T(t) = f_Y(t/60) — no division.
Right: f_T(t) = f_Y(t/60) / 60. Without the /60, the PDF no longer integrates to 1. In practice: if you're just plotting a histogram of simulated samples, multiply the samples by 60 and use density=True — numpy handles the normalization automatically.

5. Mean ≠ mode on a skewed distribution

Wrong: "the average wait is about 21 minutes" (that's the mode).
Right: mode ≈ 21 min, mean ≈ 25.35 min, median ≈ 23.93 min. All three differ on a right-skewed distribution. Annotate all three on the PDF plot.

Quick-Fire Quiz

Q1

F̄(0.5) = 0.335. What does this mean in plain English?

Reveal answer

33.5% of owls have NOT been heard by the 30-minute mark. Equivalently, 66.5% have already called within 30 minutes. It's the probability of still waiting at t=30 min.

Q2

Why is f_Y(0) = 0? Shouldn't there be some probability of hearing the owl immediately?

Reveal answer

The PDF being zero at y=0 doesn't mean it's impossible to hear the owl early — it means the density is zero exactly at the single point y=0. This is the typical shape for a time-to-event distribution that models a physical process: the owl isn't instantaneously guaranteed to call. The density builds up as time passes, peaks (most likely around 21 min), then falls off. The probability of hearing within the first 5 minutes is the area under f from 0 to 5/60 — which is non-zero even though f(0) = 0.

Q3

Your friend says: "The mean wait is 25 minutes, so if I wait 25 minutes and haven't heard the owl, something unusual is happening." Are they right?

Reveal answer

No. F̄(25/60) = F̄(0.417) ≈ S(0.417) ≈ 0.25 — about 25% of observers will still be waiting at the 25-minute mark. That's completely normal, not unusual. The mean doesn't mean "50% chance you've heard it by now" — that's the median (≈24 min, close here but not the same). In right-skewed distributions, the mean overstates the "typical" wait. A quarter of the time you're still waiting at 25 minutes.
