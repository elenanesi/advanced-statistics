Waiting Is a Different Kind of Question

Hootsworth: Task 1 had two outcomes you could count on your fingers. Here the answer is a duration — 3 minutes, 22 minutes, 1.4 hours. Between any two times there are infinitely many others. That changes the machinery.

The family of models that answers "how long until something happens?" is called survival analysis. Same maths, many names:

  • Reliability engineering: how long until this pump fails?
  • Medicine: how long until the patient relapses?
  • Marketing / subscriptions: how long until this customer churns?
  • Your task: how long until the owl calls?
Every one of those asks the same core question: at time y, what is the probability the event has not happened yet? That function of y is the survival function. Your exam hands it to you and asks you to reconstruct everything else from it.

Survival Function vs CDF — Mirror Images

Two equivalent ways to describe how probability spreads over time. Knowing one gives you the other for free.

$$\bar{F}_Y(y) = 1 - F_Y(y) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad F_Y(y) = 1 - \bar{F}_Y(y)$$

They always add to 1, because at any instant either you have heard the owl or you have not — no third option.

Why does the exam give you the survival function rather than the CDF? Because for waiting problems it is the more natural object. "What fraction of listeners are still waiting after 20 minutes?" is the question people actually ask. It is also the form in which the elegant shortcuts (slide 9) are written.

The 0.99 Problem — and Its Exact Fix

Before any calculus, one sanity check. Plug $y = 0$ into the survival function as the generator gave it to you:

$$\bar{F}(0) = 0.66\,e^{0} + 0.33\,e^{0} = 0.66 + 0.33 = 0.99 \;\neq\; 1$$

Why F̄(0) = 1 is not negotiable

$\bar{F}(0)$ is the probability that you are still waiting at the instant you open the window. That has to be certainty — it is 1 by definition. Anything less means something impossible has been smuggled in.

A missing 0.01 at the origin is a 1% atom at y = 0 — the claim that on 1 night in 100 the owl is already audible the instant the window opens, with zero waiting time. That is not the model the task describes. The second symptom is equally fatal: the density would integrate to 0.99, not 1.

Where the 0.01 actually came from

Not a trick question — a truncation artefact. The task sheet requires $\xi_5 + \xi_7 = 1$. The generator drew a split and reported each weight to two decimal places, so 0.66 and 0.33 are truncated versions of numbers that did sum to 1. The fix is to restore the ratio the generator intended, not to invent new numbers.
$$w_1 = \frac{0.66}{0.66 + 0.33} = \frac{0.66}{0.99} = \frac{2}{3}, \qquad w_2 = \frac{0.33}{0.99} = \frac{1}{3}$$ $$w_1 + w_2 = 1 \quad\checkmark \qquad \bar{F}(0) = \tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{1}{3} = 1 \quad\checkmark$$
The renormalisation lands on exactly 2/3 and 1/3 — not 0.6667 and 0.3333, but the exact rationals. That is strong evidence the underlying split really was 2:1 and only the printing was lossy. Say this in the notebook: state the given values, show $0.66+0.33 = 0.99$, explain what an atom at 0 would mean, renormalise, and note that every downstream quantity uses $w_1 = 2/3$, $w_2 = 1/3$.
Do not silently proceed with 0.66 / 0.33. If you do, your density integrates to 0.99, your quartiles have to be defined against 0.99 instead of 1, and every probability you report is quietly 1% too small. The examiner is checking whether you noticed.

From Survival Curve to Density

Hootsworth: Picture a tank of water. The level in the tank is F̄(y) — the share of listeners still waiting. The rate at which water is leaving right now is the density f(y). Draining fast means lots of owls being heard in that moment.

Level = survival. Drain rate = density. The steeper the drop between two tanks, the higher the density in between.

The formula, and the minus sign

$$f_Y(y) = -\frac{d}{dy}\,\bar{F}_Y(y)$$ $\bar{F}$ only ever goes down, so its derivative is negative everywhere. The minus sign flips it back to a positive density.

Concretely: if F̄ drops from 0.42 to 0.21 between 30 and 45 minutes, then 21% of listeners hear the owl inside that quarter-hour, and the density over that stretch is correspondingly high.

Deriving the Density — Step by Step

Start from the normalised survival function: $$\bar{F}(y) = \tfrac{2}{3}\,e^{-8y^2} + \tfrac{1}{3}\,e^{-5y^8}$$ Differentiate each exponential with the chain rule. The pattern is always $\tfrac{d}{dy}e^{-c y^{n}} = -c\,n\,y^{\,n-1}e^{-c y^{n}}$: $\tfrac{d}{dy}\bigl[e^{-8y^2}\bigr] = -16\,y\,e^{-8y^2}$ (c = 8, n = 2 → 8·2 = 16) $\tfrac{d}{dy}\bigl[e^{-5y^8}\bigr] = -40\,y^{7}e^{-5y^8}$ (c = 5, n = 8 → 5·8 = 40) Now apply $f(y) = -\tfrac{d}{dy}\bar{F}(y)$ — the two minus signs cancel: $$f_Y(y) = \tfrac{2}{3}\cdot 16\,y\,e^{-8y^2} \;+\; \tfrac{1}{3}\cdot 40\,y^{7}e^{-5y^8}$$ $$\boxed{\,f_Y(y) = \tfrac{32}{3}\,y\,e^{-8y^{2}} \;+\; \tfrac{40}{3}\,y^{7}e^{-5y^{8}}\,}$$

Spot check — does it integrate to 1?

Standard result: $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty c\,n\,y^{\,n-1}e^{-c y^{n}}\,dy = 1$ for any $c, n > 0$. $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty \tfrac{32}{3}\,y\,e^{-8y^2}\,dy = \tfrac{2}{3}\cdot 1 = \tfrac{2}{3}$ ✓ $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty \tfrac{40}{3}\,y^{7}e^{-5y^8}\,dy = \tfrac{1}{3}\cdot 1 = \tfrac{1}{3}$ ✓ Total $= \tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{1}{3} = 1$ ✓ — this is the check that would have failed (0.99) had you skipped the renormalisation.
Each bracket $c\,n\,y^{n-1}e^{-cy^{n}}$ is exactly a Weibull density in disguise. Your $f_Y$ is a weighted average of two of them: 2/3 of a Weibull with shape 2 (the Rayleigh case) and 1/3 of a Weibull with shape 8.

Why the Density Has TWO Humps

Hootsworth: There are two of me. One is an opportunist — calls whenever something stirs, usually early. The other runs on a schedule — silent for ages, then calls almost exactly on cue at about 48 minutes. Two thirds of nights you get the opportunist.

That is what a mixture means: on any given night nature first picks a mechanism (2/3 vs 1/3), then draws a waiting time from that mechanism. Two mechanisms with different typical timings ⇒ two humps.

Solid teal = the density you actually observe. The two dashed curves are the mechanisms hiding inside it.

The Trick: E[Y] Is the Area Under F̄

The usual route to a mean is $\int_0^\infty y\,f(y)\,dy$. For a non-negative variable there is a shortcut that skips the density entirely:

$$E[Y] = \int_0^\infty \bar{F}_Y(y)\,dy \qquad \text{(valid whenever } Y \geq 0\text{)}$$

Why it is true — the waiting-room picture

Hootsworth: Put 1000 people at 1000 windows. At any moment y, exactly F̄(y)·1000 of them are still waiting. Add up "people still waiting" over every instant and you get the total person-hours of waiting. Divide by 1000 and you have the average wait.

Every thin vertical strip contributes (fraction still waiting) × (width of the instant). Summing the strips is exactly the integral.

The one-line algebraic proof
$$\int_0^\infty \bar{F}(y)\,dy = \int_0^\infty\!\!\int_y^\infty f(t)\,dt\,dy = \int_0^\infty\!\!\int_0^t f(t)\,dy\,dt = \int_0^\infty t\,f(t)\,dt = E[Y]$$

All that happened is swapping the order of integration over the region $0 \le y \le t$. The same move applied to $\int_0^\infty y\,\bar{F}(y)\,dy$ gives the second moment: $E[Y^2] = 2\int_0^\infty y\,\bar{F}(y)\,dy$.

This is the reason survival functions are convenient. Your $\bar{F}$ is a clean sum of two exponentials; your density is a messier sum of two products. Integrating the clean one is far easier — and it is why the closed forms on the next slide exist at all.

Mean and Variance in Closed Form

Apply the trick term by term. Both integrals are standard, with $a = 8$ (the y² rate) and $b = 5$ (the y⁸ rate):

$$E[Y] = \int_0^\infty \bar{F}(y)\,dy = w_1\underbrace{\int_0^\infty\! e^{-ay^2}dy}_{\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\pi/a}} \;+\; w_2\underbrace{\int_0^\infty\! e^{-by^8}dy}_{\Gamma(9/8)\,b^{-1/8}}$$ $$E[Y] = w_1\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{a}} \;+\; w_2\,\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{9}{8}\right)b^{-1/8}$$
$$E[Y^2] = 2\int_0^\infty y\,\bar{F}(y)\,dy = \frac{w_1}{a} \;+\; \frac{w_2\,\Gamma(1/4)}{4\,b^{1/4}}$$

The Gamma function shows up because the y⁸ exponent forces a substitution $u = by^8$, and the leftover integral is precisely the definition of Γ.

Also mind the units: a variance in hours² becomes minutes² by multiplying by 60² = 3600, but a standard deviation only by 60. That asymmetry catches people every year.

P(2 < Y < 4) — and Why It Is Effectively Zero

The survival function makes interval probabilities almost free. No integration required:

$$P(a < Y < b) = P(Y > a) - P(Y > b) = \bar{F}(a) - \bar{F}(b)$$

The calculation

$\bar{F}(2) = \tfrac23 e^{-8\cdot 4} + \tfrac13 e^{-5\cdot 256} = \tfrac23 e^{-32} + \tfrac13 e^{-1280}$ $= \tfrac23(1.27\times 10^{-14}) + (\text{underflows to }0) = 8.44\times 10^{-15}$ $\bar{F}(4) = \tfrac23 e^{-128} + \tfrac13 e^{-327680} \approx 1.6\times 10^{-56} \approx 0$ $$P(2 < Y < 4) = \bar{F}(2) - \bar{F}(4) = 8.44\times 10^{-15}$$

Why so astronomically small?

Because the model has already spent all of its probability:

  • $P(Y < 1\text{ hour}) = 0.99753$ — 99.75% of the mass is inside the first hour.
  • The tail is Gaussian-squared fast: at $y = 2$ the first term carries $e^{-32}$, and the second carries $e^{-1280}$, which is smaller than any number a float can represent.
  • For comparison, $8.44\times 10^{-15}$ is roughly the chance of picking one pre-chosen second out of the next 2.7 million years.
This is the right answer, not a bug. Write it as such: "The result is 8.44 × 10⁻¹⁵, i.e. numerically indistinguishable from zero. This is a genuine property of the model: with rates 8 and 5 on y² and y⁸, the distribution places 99.75% of its mass below one hour, so the window from 2 to 4 hours lies far beyond the effective support."
Do not report "0" with no comment, and do not claim the model is broken. Reporting the magnitude and explaining it is the graded part.

Hours → Minutes: the Unit Trap

Your survival function is defined in hours. The task explicitly asks for a minute-level histogram, and explicitly warns about the conversion. Get this wrong and every number on the plot is off by a factor of 60.

Two things scale differently

$$f_T(t) = f_Y\!\left(\tfrac{t}{60}\right)\cdot\left|\frac{dy}{dt}\right| = \frac{f_Y(t/60)}{60}$$ The $\tfrac{1}{60}$ is the Jacobian — without it the "density" would integrate to 60. Survival functions, in contrast, need no correction: $\bar{F}_T(t) = P(T > t) = P(60Y > t) = P\!\left(Y > \tfrac{t}{60}\right) = \bar{F}_Y\!\left(\tfrac{t}{60}\right)$

That asymmetry is the point: a probability is a probability in any unit, so $\bar{F}$ just takes a rescaled argument. A density is probability per unit time, so changing the unit changes its height.

Practical shortcut: if you are plotting a histogram of simulated draws, sample in hours, multiply the samples by 60, and let density=True handle the normalisation. The Jacobian is applied for you. You still need to understand it — the exam may ask you to state $f_T$.
SUB-CHAPTER

Why the ÷60 Has to Be There

The Jacobian feels like bookkeeping. Here is why it is forced on you.

"The owl calls between 0.25 h and 0.35 h" and "the owl calls between 15 min and 21 min" are the same physical event, so they must carry the same probability. Probability is the area of the strip. Measured in minutes the strip is 60 times wider, so its height must be 60 times shorter for the area to survive the translation.

General rule: for $T = cY$ with $c > 0$, $f_T(t) = f_Y(t/c)/c$. The $1/c$ exists purely to defend the area.

The Minute-by-Minute Histogram

The cleanest way to build it needs no simulation at all. The probability that the owl is first heard during minute k is the drop in the survival function across that minute:

$$P(\text{first heard in minute } k) = \bar{F}\!\left(\tfrac{k-1}{60}\right) - \bar{F}\!\left(\tfrac{k}{60}\right), \qquad k = 1, 2, \ldots, 60$$

Both humps survive the unit conversion. They have to — changing units cannot change which waits are common.

Common Pitfalls

1. Using 0.66 / 0.33 without renormalising

Wrong: proceeding with F̄(0) = 0.99.
Right: flag it, explain that F̄(0) < 1 implies a 1% atom at y = 0 and a density integrating to 0.99, then renormalise to exactly w₁ = 2/3, w₂ = 1/3.

2. Wrong sign on the derivative

Wrong: f(y) = d/dy F̄(y) → a negative "density".
Right: f(y) = −d/dy F̄(y). F̄ is decreasing, so the minus sign is what makes f positive.

3. Dropping the exponent when differentiating y⁸

Wrong: d/dy e^(−5y⁸) = −5y⁷e^(−5y⁸) (forgot the factor 8).
Right: −40y⁷e^(−5y⁸). The rule is d/dy e^(−cyⁿ) = −c·n·y^(n−1)·e^(−cyⁿ), so the coefficient is 5 × 8 = 40, and likewise 8 × 2 = 16 for the first term.

4. Reporting only the mean on a bimodal distribution

Wrong: "the typical wait is about 28 minutes."
Right: 27.94 min is the mean, but it lands in the valley between two humps at 15 min and 48 min. Report both modes, the median (24.90) and the IQR (28.24), and say the distribution is bimodal.

5. Presenting P(2 < Y < 4) without interpretation

Wrong: printing 8.44e-15 and moving on, or writing "0".
Right: report the magnitude and explain it — 99.75% of the mass is under one hour, so 2–4 hours is deep in a doubly-exponential tail.

6. Forgetting the unit conversion (or applying it twice)

Wrong: plotting f_Y on a minutes axis, or dividing simulated samples by 60 instead of multiplying.
Right: T = 60Y. Survival takes a rescaled argument, F̄_T(t) = F̄_Y(t/60); density also gains the ÷60 Jacobian, f_T(t) = f_Y(t/60)/60.

Quick-Fire Quiz

Q1

F̄(0.5) = 0.417. Say it in plain English, twice — once from each side.

Reveal answer

41.7% of listeners are still waiting at the 30-minute mark. Equivalently, 58.3% have already heard the owl by then. Both statements describe the same number; the survival function reports the first, the CDF the second.

Q2

Someone hands you the original weights and says "0.99 is close enough to 1, just carry on". Give the two concrete things that break.

Reveal answer

(1) F̄(0) = 0.99 means P(Y = 0) = 0.01 — a 1% atom at the origin, i.e. the claim that on 1 night in 100 the owl is already calling at the instant you open the window. The task describes a waiting time, not that. (2) The density then integrates to 0.99 instead of 1, so it is not a valid probability density at all, and every quantile would have to be defined against 0.99. Renormalising to 2/3 and 1/3 fixes both at once and is exact, not approximate.

Q3

Why does the density have a hump at 48 minutes when the first component has practically died out by then?

Reveal answer

Because the second component is a Weibull with shape 8. Its instantaneous risk stays near zero for a long time and then rises extremely steeply, so its probability is concentrated in a narrow window around 0.80 h ≈ 48 min. It carries weight 1/3, which is more than enough to build a visible second hump in a region where component 1 contributes almost nothing. Mechanistically: one third of nights follow a "clockwork" owl that calls almost on schedule.

Q4

Your friend converts to minutes and writes f_T(15) = f_Y(0.25) = 1.618. What went wrong, and what is the right value?

Reveal answer

They forgot the Jacobian. A density is probability per unit of time, so its height depends on the unit. The correct value is f_T(15) = f_Y(0.25)/60 = 1.618/60 = 0.02697 per minute. Sanity check: a density of 1.618 per minute would imply more than 100% probability inside a single minute.
