Meet S — read the density like a picture

$S$ = how many terabytes a single router pushes through before its hardware fails. It's a continuous random variable, so it gets a PDF. Before any algebra, look at what the formula's two ingredients do:

$$f_S(s) = \frac{1}{720\,\theta^{7}}\; \underbrace{s^{6}}_{\text{rises}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{e^{-s/\theta}}_{\text{falls}} \qquad (s \ge 0)$$ Heads-up: the exam sheet prints 120, not 720 — that's a typo we'll flag properly in a moment.
  • $s^6$ says: instant deaths are essentially impossible. Near $s=0$ the density is crushed to zero — a router basically never fails after moving only a few gigabytes.
  • $e^{-s/\theta}$ says: surviving forever is impossible too. Every extra terabyte costs a constant multiplicative "wear tax".
  • Their product is a hump: failure most plausibly happens in a middle zone — this is the Gamma distribution shape.

Concretely: this is a Gamma distribution with shape $k=7$ and scale $\theta$, written $S \sim \Gamma(7, \theta)$.

θ — the one knob we don't know

The shape (the exponent 6, i.e. $k=7$) is fixed by the model — the exam hands it to you. The scale $\theta$ is the unknown: it says how many TB one "unit of wear" is worth. Same silhouette, different stretch:

mode (peak)  = $(k-1)\,\theta = 6\theta$
mean $E[S]$  = $k\,\theta = 7\theta$
variance     = $k\,\theta^2 = 7\theta^2$

Turn $\theta$ up → routers are tougher, the hump slides right and flattens. Everything in Q3/Q4 is about using data to find where this knob is set.

SUB-CHAPTER

Why shape 7? The 7-stage story

A Gamma distribution with an integer shape $k$ has a beautiful physical reading (this special case is called the Erlang distribution):

The intuition Imagine the router's life as 7 internal wear stages. Each stage lasts an Exponential$(\theta)$ amount of traffic — memoryless, average $\theta$ TB. The router dies when the 7th stage completes. Total life = sum of 7 exponential waits = $\Gamma(7,\theta)$.
  • $\xi_9=0$ branch: $k=1$ (plain Exponential — 1 stage). $\xi_9=1$: $k=5$. Yours ($\xi_9=2$): $k=7$.
  • This story is not just decoration — it's the exact reason the dual-router result on the "shapes add" slide will feel obvious instead of magical.

⚠ Flag: the exam's 120 should be 720

Packet: Remember Task 2's "0.79 + 0.20 = 0.99" trap? Same energy here. A density must enclose exactly area 1 — and the exam's constant doesn't.

Every PDF must satisfy $\int_0^\infty f_S(s)\,ds = 1$. The general Gamma density uses the Gamma function $\Gamma(k)=(k-1)!$ as its normalizer. Check the exam's three branches against that:

SUB-CHAPTER

The area check, step by step

Two lines, and it's exam-proof material (Q1 asks you to justify the model). Substitute $u = s/\theta$ (so $s = \theta u$, $ds = \theta\,du$) to peel $\theta$ out of the integral:

$$\int_0^\infty s^6 e^{-s/\theta}\,ds \;=\; \int_0^\infty (\theta u)^6 e^{-u}\,\theta\,du \;=\; \theta^7 \int_0^\infty u^6 e^{-u}\,du \;=\; \theta^7\,\Gamma(7) \;=\; 720\,\theta^7$$

So the correctly normalized density divides by exactly what the integral produces:

$$f_S(s) = \frac{s^6 e^{-s/\theta}}{720\,\theta^7} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \int_0^\infty f_S(s)\,ds = \frac{720\,\theta^7}{720\,\theta^7} = 1$$ ✔ area is exactly 1

The same one-substitution trick ("pull out $\theta$, recognize $\Gamma(\cdot)$") also delivers the mean: $E[S] = \frac{1}{720\theta^7}\int_0^\infty s^7 e^{-s/\theta} ds = \frac{\Gamma(8)\,\theta^8}{720\,\theta^7} = \frac{5040}{720}\theta = 7\theta$. That's the $k\theta$ rule you saw two slides ago — now proven, not just stated.

Two routers, one number: T = S₁ + S₂

The dual system: router 1 carries traffic until it dies; a standby router 2 takes over automatically and carries traffic until it dies. The system's total bandwidth-to-failure is:

$$T = S_1 + S_2$$

The "realistic assumptions" the exam wants — spelled out

  • Independence: $S_1$ and $S_2$ are separate physical machines; one wearing out doesn't age the other. (Realistic unless they share a power supply or overheating rack — worth one honest sentence in the notebook.)
  • Identically distributed: "two routers of the same type" → same model, same $\theta$: both $\Gamma(7,\theta)$. Together with independence this is the classic i.i.d. setup.
  • Instant failover: router 2 takes over with no gap and no traffic lost, and it doesn't age while on standby — so the total is exactly the sum, nothing more.

In course-book language, $T$ is a transformed random variable (Unit 3.5): the transformation is $g(s_1, s_2) = s_1 + s_2$ applied to the random pair $(S_1, S_2)$.

The density of T: shapes add

Here's the punchline first, story second, proof in the sub-chapter:

$$S_1, S_2 \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \Gamma(7,\theta) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T = S_1 + S_2 \sim \Gamma(14,\theta)$$ $$f_T(t) = \frac{1}{13!\,\theta^{14}}\; t^{13}\, e^{-t/\theta} \qquad (t \ge 0), \qquad 13! = 6{,}227{,}020{,}800$$

Why it's believable without algebra: remember the 7-stage story. Router 1 = 7 exponential wear stages, router 2 = 7 more. The system just walks through 14 stages end to end — and a sum of 14 i.i.d. Exponential$(\theta)$ chunks is $\Gamma(14,\theta)$ by the very same story.

When "shapes add" is allowed — don't skip this check $\Gamma(k_1,\theta) + \Gamma(k_2,\theta) = \Gamma(k_1{+}k_2,\theta)$ requires (1) independence and (2) the same scale $\theta$. Different scales → the sum is not Gamma at all. Here both hold by our assumptions — say so explicitly in the notebook.

Useful immediately: $E[T] = 14\,\theta$ and $\operatorname{Var}[T] = 14\,\theta^2$.

SUB-CHAPTER

Proof: transformation theorem → convolution

The exam says "calculate the density function of $T$" — the course book's tool is the transformation theorem (Unit 3.5, Eq. 3.39; Hogg et al. 2020, §2.7). Strategy: sums lose information (you can't recover $s_1, s_2$ from $t$ alone), so add a helper coordinate, transform the pair, then integrate the helper away.

  1. Choose the transformation. $g(s_1,s_2) = (t, u) = (s_1{+}s_2,\; s_2)$. Inverse: $s_1 = t-u$, $s_2 = u$.
  2. Compute the Jacobian. $J = \det\begin{pmatrix} \partial s_1/\partial t & \partial s_1/\partial u \\ \partial s_2/\partial t & \partial s_2/\partial u \end{pmatrix} = \det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 1$ — a sum doesn't stretch area.
  3. Joint density in, marginalize the helper out. Independence gives $f_{(S_1,S_2)}(s_1,s_2) = f_S(s_1)f_S(s_2)$, so $$f_T(t) = \int_0^{t} f_S(t-u)\,f_S(u)\,du$$ — this integral is called the convolution of $f_S$ with itself. Limits $0$ to $t$: both pieces must be non-negative.
  4. Plug in and watch $u$ vanish from the exponent — the step that only works because both routers share the same $\theta$: $$f_T(t) = \frac{e^{-t/\theta}}{720^2\,\theta^{14}} \int_0^{t} (t-u)^6\, u^6\, du$$ since $e^{-(t-u)/\theta}\,e^{-u/\theta} = e^{-t/\theta}$.
  5. The remaining integral is a classic. Substitute $u = tv$: $\int_0^t (t-u)^6 u^6\,du = t^{13}\int_0^1 (1-v)^6 v^6\,dv = t^{13}\,\frac{6!\,6!}{13!}$ (that last value is the Beta function $B(7,7)$).
$$f_T(t) = \frac{e^{-t/\theta}}{6!\,6!\,\theta^{14}}\cdot t^{13}\,\frac{6!\,6!}{13!} = \frac{t^{13}\,e^{-t/\theta}}{13!\,\theta^{14}} \;\;=\;\; \Gamma(14,\theta)\ \text{density}$$ ✔ exactly the Gamma(14, θ) form

Exactly the "shapes add" result — now with a citation-ready derivation for the notebook.

Likelihood: guess-and-compare

The infrastructure team ran the experiment: five dual-router systems, run to failure. Your data (values of $T$, in TB):

$t_1..t_5$ = 37,  58,  8,  176,  10   (sample size $n=5$)

The idea before the formula: pick a candidate $\theta$ and ask, "if the world really had this $\theta$, how plausible would my exact five numbers be?" Try many candidates; crown the one that makes the data most plausible. That plausibility-as-a-function-of-$\theta$ is the likelihood:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{5} f_T(t_i \mid \theta)$$ product because the five systems are independent — joint density = product of densities (course book Eq. 6.6)

$\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ for YOUR five data points, computed honestly — not a sketch. The curve is razor-thin: bad guesses are punished brutally.

Two lookalikes — keep them apart Likelihood ≠ probability of θ. $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = P(\text{data} \mid \theta)$, read as a function of $\theta$ with the data frozen. It is NOT $P(\theta \mid \text{data})$ — that reversed reading is Bayesian and is exactly what Task 6 will do.  Also: use $f_T$, not $f_S$ — the sample is five dual-system totals, so each observation follows $\Gamma(14,\theta)$, never $\Gamma(7,\theta)$.

The exam's "transformation": take the log

Q3's second half — "propose a transformation of this likelihood function whose maximum is the same and can be computed easily" — has a one-word answer: logarithm. The log-likelihood is $\ell(\theta) = \ln \mathcal{L}(\theta)$ (course book Eq. 6.7). Three reasons, all exam-quotable:

  1. Same maximum. $\ln$ is strictly monotonic, so the peak stays at the same $\theta$ — only its height changes.
  2. Products become sums. $\ln \prod = \sum \ln$. Differentiating a 5-term product needs the product rule five ways; differentiating a sum is term-by-term.
  3. Computers need it. For your data the peak value is $\mathcal{L}(\hat\theta) \approx 5.8 \times 10^{-26}$. With more data, that races toward underflow. Its log is a tame $-58.1$.

One more convention worth a sentence in the notebook: optimizers minimize rather than maximize, so software works with the negative log-likelihood $-\ell(\theta)$ (course book Eq. 6.9). Same location, flipped upside down.

Maximize: five lines of calculus

Write out $\ell(\theta)$ by taking logs of $f_T(t_i) = \frac{t_i^{13} e^{-t_i/\theta}}{13!\,\theta^{14}}$ and summing over the 5 observations:

$$\ell(\theta) = 13\sum_i \ln t_i \;-\; \frac{1}{\theta}\sum_i t_i \;-\; n\ln 13! \;-\; 14\,n \ln \theta$$ only the middle two terms contain θ — the rest is dead weight that the derivative will erase

Differentiate with respect to $\theta$ (recall $\frac{d}{d\theta}\frac{1}{\theta} = -\frac{1}{\theta^2}$ and $\frac{d}{d\theta}\ln\theta = \frac{1}{\theta}$), set to zero:

$$\ell'(\theta) = \frac{\sum_i t_i}{\theta^2} - \frac{14n}{\theta} = 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \frac{\sum_i t_i}{\theta} = 14n \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \boxed{\hat\theta = \frac{\sum_i t_i}{14\,n} = \frac{\bar t}{14}}$$
  • Confirm it's a peak, not a valley: $\ell''(\theta) = -\frac{2\sum t_i}{\theta^3} + \frac{14n}{\theta^2}$, and at $\theta = \hat\theta$ this equals $-\frac{14n}{\hat\theta^2} < 0$ ✔ (downward curvature = maximum). One line, but graders love it.
  • The general pattern (worth memorizing for Task 6 too): for $\Gamma(k, \theta)$ with known shape $k$, the MLE of the scale is always $\hat\theta = \bar x / k$ — "sample mean divided by shape".

Plug in your numbers

$$\hat\theta = \frac{289}{14 \times 5} = \frac{289}{70} \approx 4.129 \text{ TB} \qquad\qquad E[T] = 14\,\hat\theta = \frac{289}{5} = 57.8 \text{ TB}$$
Notice what just happened $E[T] = 14 \times \frac{\bar t}{14} = \bar t$ — the fitted model's mean IS the sample mean, exactly. That's not a coincidence and not circular reasoning: maximum likelihood, for this family, chooses to calibrate the model so its average matches the data's average. The estimate of the single-router mean follows for free: $E[S] = 7\hat\theta = 28.9$ TB.

Honest caveat for the notebook: $n = 5$ is tiny and the 176 carries a lot of weight — the estimate is exactly right given the model, but a fresh sample of 5 could move it substantially.

Watch out! Five traps

  1. Fitting $f_S$ to $T$-data. The five numbers are dual-system totals → use $\Gamma(14,\theta)$. Using $\Gamma(7,\theta)$ by reflex gives $\hat\theta = \bar t/7 \approx 8.26$ — double the right answer, and silently wrong.
  2. Using the exam's 120 without flagging it. It doesn't move $\hat\theta$, but an unnormalized "density" fails the acceptance criteria (proof steps + tool trust). Flag → correct to 720 → proceed.
  3. Scale vs. rate. Our $\theta$ multiplies (scale). Many texts write Gamma with a rate $\lambda = 1/\theta$ that divides. In scipy: gamma(a=14, scale=theta) — passing a rate where scale belongs inverts your answer.
  4. $\theta$ vs. $\hat\theta$. $\theta$ is the unknown true knob; $\hat\theta = 4.129$ is your data-driven estimate of it. Keep the hat on in the write-up — graders read it as "I know the difference".
  5. Units. $\xi_{10}$ is in terabytes, so $\hat\theta$ and $E[T]$ are in TB too. Label the axes (acceptance criterion: "reader can read values").

Quiz

Q1. A classmate fits the single-router density to the five numbers and proudly reports $\hat\theta \approx 8.26$ TB. What went wrong, and why is the error exactly a factor of 2?

Reveal answer

They used $f_S$ (shape 7) instead of $f_T$ (shape 14). The scale MLE is $\bar t / k$, so halving $k$ from 14 to 7 doubles the estimate: $57.8/7 \approx 8.26$ vs the correct $57.8/14 \approx 4.13$.

Q2. The exam asks for "a transformation whose maximum is the same". Why does taking the log guarantee the maximum location doesn't move?

Reveal answer

Because $\ln$ is strictly increasing: if $\mathcal{L}(\theta_1) > \mathcal{L}(\theta_2)$ then $\ln\mathcal{L}(\theta_1) > \ln\mathcal{L}(\theta_2)$ — every comparison between candidates is preserved, so the winner is preserved. Only heights change, never the ranking.

Q3. Without redoing any calculus: if the infrastructure team had measured 5 single routers instead (same numbers), what would $\hat\theta$ be?

Reveal answer

$\hat\theta = \bar t / 7 = 57.8 / 7 \approx 8.26$ TB — same "mean over shape" rule, shape 7 this time. (Same arithmetic as Q1's mistake, but now it would be correct, because the model would match the data's origin.)

Q4. True or false: because the exam's constant 120 is wrong, the maximum likelihood estimate computed from it is also wrong.

Reveal answer

False. The constant multiplies $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ by the same factor ($6^n$) for every $\theta$ — in log form it adds a constant, which the derivative kills. $\hat\theta$ is untouched. But probabilities, plots, and $\int f = 1$ checks WOULD all be wrong, which is why you still must flag and fix it.
