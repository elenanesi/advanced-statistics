Meet S — an odometer, not a clock

First the picture. Imagine each router has an odometer that counts terabytes pushed through it, not hours lived. One day the hardware quits and you read the odometer. That reading is $S$.

So $S$ is a continuous quantity in TB, it can never be negative, and it is random — build two identical routers and they will die at two different odometer readings. Continuous and random means it gets a PDF. The exam hands you this one:

$$f_S(s) \;=\; \frac{1}{\theta}\,e^{-s/\theta}, \qquad s > 0$$ this is the Exponential distribution with mean θ, written $S \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\theta)$

The exponential density, drawn at $\theta = 10.6$ TB — the value you will fit later in this deck.

Two lookalikes: "most likely" vs "on average" The curve is highest at $s = 0$, so the mode (most likely neighbourhood) is right at the start. But the mean is $\theta$, well to the right, because the long thin tail drags the average out. These two words describe different things and for the exponential they are as far apart as they can get. Saying "the router usually fails around $\theta$" is wrong; saying "it fails at $\theta$ on average" is right.

Why exponential? The router that never ages

Before the formula: the exponential is the model you choose when failure is caused by random shocks — a voltage spike, a bad packet storm, a cosmic-ray bit flip — and not by parts slowly wearing down.

Concretely, that means a router with 20 TB already on the odometer is statistically indistinguishable from a brand-new one. It has not "used up" any of its luck. The property has a name:

$$P(S > a + b \mid S > a) \;=\; P(S > b) \qquad \text{for all } a, b > 0$$ the memoryless property — the exponential is the only continuous distribution that has it
Say this honestly in the notebook Memorylessness is a modelling assumption, not a fact about routers. Real hardware often has a wear-out phase where the failure rate climbs with age; the exponential explicitly rules that out (it is the constant-hazard model). It is a defensible first model for electronics dominated by random shocks, and the exam prescribes it — but a grader rewards you for naming the limitation rather than pretending it is not there.

θ — the one knob we don't know

The exponential has exactly one parameter. $\theta$ is a scale: it says how many terabytes one "unit of luck" is worth. Turn it up and the whole curve stretches to the right — same shape, tougher routers.

mean $E[S]$    = $\theta$
standard deviation = $\theta$  (yes, the same number — see below)
median        = $\theta\ln 2 \approx 0.693\,\theta$
mode          = $0$

That mean-equals-standard-deviation fact is a free diagnostic. Its scale-free version, the coefficient of variation, equals exactly 1 for any exponential. We will use it as a model check at the end.

Everything from here on is about locating this one knob from data.

Two routers, one number: T = S₁ + S₂

The dual system: router 1 carries traffic until it dies; a standby router 2 comes up automatically and carries traffic until it dies. The system's total bandwidth-to-failure is what the exam calls $T$:

$$T = S_1 + S_2$$

The "realistic assumptions" the exam wants — spelled out

  • Independence. $S_1$ and $S_2$ are separate physical machines; one wearing out does not influence the other. (Honest caveat: they would not be independent if they shared a power supply or an overheating rack. Write that sentence.)
  • Identically distributed. "Two routers of the same type" means the same model with the same $\theta$: both $\mathrm{Exp}(\theta)$. Together with independence this is the classic i.i.d. setup.
  • Instantaneous, perfect failover. Router 2 takes over with no gap and no traffic lost, so the total is exactly the sum and nothing is added or dropped at the handover.
  • No ageing on standby. Router 2 does not degrade while waiting. This is precisely the memoryless property from the previous slide, reused — which is why the exponential makes this system easy to reason about.

In course-book language, $T$ is a transformed random variable (Unit 3.5): the transformation is $g(s_1, s_2) = s_1 + s_2$ applied to the random pair $(S_1, S_2)$.

Counting the ways to split a total

Here is the whole derivation as a picture, before any integral. Suppose the system delivered exactly $t = 20$ TB. How could that have happened? Router 1 could have done 2 and router 2 did 18. Or 9 and 11. Or 17 and 3. Every split adds up to the same 20.

The cancellation on the middle line is special to the exponential and it is the entire trick. Every way of splitting the total is equally plausible. So the only thing that varies with $t$ is how much room there is to split it — and a total of $t$ has exactly $t$ worth of room. That gives a factor of $t$ out front:

$$f_T(t) \;=\; \underbrace{t}_{\text{room to split}} \;\times\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}e^{-t/\theta}}_{\text{weight of each split}} \;=\; \frac{t}{\theta^{2}}\,e^{-t/\theta}, \qquad t > 0$$

The formal name for "sum over all the ways to split" is convolution. The sub-chapter does it as an integral, but the integral is only this picture written in symbols.

SUB-CHAPTER

The convolution integral, line by line

The exam says "calculate the density function of $T$", so the notebook needs the symbols, not just the picture. The course-book tool is the transformation theorem (Unit 3.5; Hogg, McKean & Craig 2020, §2.7). A sum loses information — you cannot recover $s_1$ and $s_2$ from $t$ alone — so the standard move is: add a helper coordinate, transform the pair, then integrate the helper away.

  1. Choose the transformation. $g(s_1,s_2) = (t,u) = (s_1{+}s_2,\; s_2)$, with inverse $s_1 = t-u$, $s_2 = u$.
  2. Compute the Jacobian. $J = \det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 1$ — adding does not stretch area, so no correction factor appears.
  3. Factor the joint density, then marginalise. Independence gives $f_{(S_1,S_2)}(s_1,s_2) = f_S(s_1)f_S(s_2)$, so $$f_T(t) \;=\; \int_0^{t} f_S(u)\,f_S(t-u)\,du$$ The limits run from $0$ to $t$ because both pieces must be non-negative and must still sum to $t$.
  4. Substitute the exponential and watch $u$ vanish. $$f_T(t) = \int_0^{t} \frac{1}{\theta}e^{-u/\theta}\cdot\frac{1}{\theta}e^{-(t-u)/\theta}\,du = \frac{1}{\theta^{2}}\,e^{-t/\theta}\int_0^{t} du$$ because $e^{-u/\theta}e^{-(t-u)/\theta} = e^{-t/\theta}$, which no longer depends on $u$.
  5. The leftover integral is just the length of the interval. $\int_0^t du = t$.
$$\boxed{\,f_T(t) = \frac{t}{\theta^{2}}\,e^{-t/\theta}\,}, \qquad t > 0$$ ✔ and it integrates to 1: $\int_0^\infty t\,e^{-t/\theta}dt = \theta^{2}$, which the $1/\theta^{2}$ cancels exactly

Step 4 is the step that requires both routers to share the same $\theta$. With two different scales the exponents do not collapse, the $u$ survives the integral, and the answer is not a Gamma density at all. That is why the equal-$\theta$ assumption on the previous slide had to be stated out loud.

The result: T is Gamma with shape 2

That density has a name. A sum of $k$ independent exponentials with a shared scale $\theta$ is a Gamma distribution with shape $k$ and scale $\theta$; when the shape is a whole number it is also called an Erlang distribution. Two routers give shape 2:

$$S_1, S_2 \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathrm{Exp}(\theta) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T = S_1 + S_2 \sim \Gamma(2,\theta)$$ $$f_T(t) = \frac{t}{\theta^{2}}e^{-t/\theta}, \qquad E[T] = 2\theta, \qquad \operatorname{Var}[T] = 2\theta^{2}$$

Read the shape change. One router is most likely to die immediately (peak at zero). Two routers in sequence are not: for the system to fail at 0 TB, both routers would have to die at once, and that is vanishingly unlikely. So $f_T(0) = 0$, the density rises to a hump at $t = \theta$, and then decays. The redundancy shows up as that hump.

The general rule, worth remembering $\Gamma(k_1,\theta) + \Gamma(k_2,\theta) = \Gamma(k_1{+}k_2,\theta)$ — shapes add, provided (1) the pieces are independent and (2) they share the same scale $\theta$. An exponential is $\Gamma(1,\theta)$, so $1 + 1 = 2$. State both conditions in the notebook; the result is false without them.

Likelihood: guess-and-compare

The infrastructure team ran the experiment: five dual-router systems, each run to failure. Your data (values of $T$, in terabytes):

$t_1 \ldots t_5 \;=\; 33,\;\; 29,\;\; 6,\;\; 37,\;\; 1$     (sample size $n = 5$)

The idea before the formula. Pick a candidate value for $\theta$ and ask: "if the world really were like this, how plausible would my exact five numbers be?" Try lots of candidates and crown the one that makes the observed data most plausible. That plausibility, read as a function of $\theta$ with the data held fixed, is the likelihood:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{5} f_T(t_i \mid \theta) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{5} \frac{t_i}{\theta^{2}}e^{-t_i/\theta} \;=\; \Big(\textstyle\prod_i t_i\Big)\,\theta^{-2n}\,\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{1}{\theta}\textstyle\sum_i t_i\Big)$$ a product because the five systems are independent — joint density = product of densities

$\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ evaluated on YOUR five numbers — computed, not sketched. Notice the peak is broad: with $n=5$ a lot of candidate values are almost as good as the winner. That vagueness comes back on the uncertainty slide.

Two lookalikes — keep them apart Likelihood is not the probability of θ. $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = P(\text{data} \mid \theta)$, read as a function of $\theta$ with the data frozen. It is not $P(\theta \mid \text{data})$; that reversed reading is the Bayesian one and is exactly what Task 6 does.  Second: use $f_T$, not $f_S$. Your five numbers are dual-system totals, so each follows $\Gamma(2,\theta)$, never $\mathrm{Exp}(\theta)$.

The exam's "transformation": take the log

Q3's second half — "propose a transformation of this likelihood function whose maximum is the same and can be computed easily" — has a one-word answer: logarithm. The log-likelihood is $\ell(\theta) = \ln \mathcal{L}(\theta)$. Three reasons, all exam-quotable:

  1. The maximum cannot move. $\ln$ is strictly monotonic, so it preserves the ranking of every pair of candidates. Whoever won before still wins. Only the height of the peak changes.
  2. Products become sums. $\ln \prod = \sum \ln$. Differentiating a five-factor product needs the product rule five ways; differentiating a sum is term by term.
  3. Computers need it. For your data the peak value is $\mathcal{L}(\hat\theta) \approx 5.4 \times 10^{-10}$. With a larger sample that races toward numerical underflow. Its logarithm is a tame $-21.3$.

One convention worth a sentence in the notebook: numerical optimisers minimise rather than maximise, so software works with the negative log-likelihood $-\ell(\theta)$. Same location, flipped upside down.

Maximise: four lines of calculus

Take the logarithm of the likelihood from two slides ago. Products become sums, powers come down as multipliers, and the exponential disappears entirely:

$$\ell(\theta) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln t_i \;-\; 2n\ln\theta \;-\; \frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{n} t_i$$ the first term has no θ in it — the derivative will delete it, so it is dead weight

Differentiate with respect to $\theta$, using $\frac{d}{d\theta}\ln\theta = \frac{1}{\theta}$ and $\frac{d}{d\theta}\frac{1}{\theta} = -\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}$, then set the result to zero:

$$\ell'(\theta) \;=\; -\frac{2n}{\theta} + \frac{\sum_i t_i}{\theta^{2}} \;=\; 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \frac{\sum_i t_i}{\theta} = 2n \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \boxed{\;\hat\theta = \frac{\sum_i t_i}{2n} = \frac{\bar t}{2}\;}$$

In words: the estimate is the sample mean divided by the shape. Two routers per observation, so divide by two. The whole of Q4 is that sentence plus arithmetic.

Hat discipline $\theta$ is the unknown true knob setting; $\hat\theta$ is your data-driven estimate of it. They are different objects and the hat is not decoration. Keep it throughout the write-up — graders read it as evidence you know the difference.
SUB-CHAPTER

Confirming it's a peak, not a valley

Setting a derivative to zero finds a flat spot. Flat spots come in three kinds: maxima, minima, and saddle points. One extra line settles which one you found, and it is the kind of line graders look for.

$$\ell''(\theta) \;=\; \frac{2n}{\theta^{2}} \;-\; \frac{2\sum_i t_i}{\theta^{3}}$$ Now substitute the stationary point, where $\sum_i t_i = 2n\hat\theta$: $$\ell''(\hat\theta) \;=\; \frac{2n}{\hat\theta^{2}} - \frac{2\cdot 2n\hat\theta}{\hat\theta^{3}} \;=\; \frac{2n}{\hat\theta^{2}} - \frac{4n}{\hat\theta^{2}} \;=\; -\frac{2n}{\hat\theta^{2}} \;<\; 0$$ ✔ negative curvature = the curve bends downward = maximum

With your numbers, $\ell''(\hat\theta) = -10/10.6^{2} = -0.0890$. It is strictly negative for every possible sample (as long as $n \ge 1$ and $\hat\theta > 0$), so the maximum is unique — there is no second candidate to worry about.

Do not throw this number away That same quantity $2n/\hat\theta^{2}$ reappears two slides later as the Fisher information, which is what turns $\hat\theta$ into a confidence interval. The curvature of the log-likelihood at its peak is the precision of the estimate: a sharp peak means the data pin $\theta$ down, a flat peak means they barely do.

Why E[T] lands exactly on the sample mean

Look again: $E[T] = 2\hat\theta = 2 \times \frac{\bar t}{2} = \bar t = 21.2$. The fitted model's mean is exactly the average of your five numbers. That looks suspicious — like the answer was assumed rather than derived. It was not.

The reason is visible in the derivative you solved. Setting $\ell'(\theta) = 0$ gave $\sum_i t_i = 2n\theta$, which is the same as $\bar t = 2\theta = E[T]$. In other words, the likelihood equation for this family literally is the statement "set the model mean equal to the sample mean". Two different estimation philosophies collide here and agree:

  • Maximum likelihood — choose $\theta$ to make the observed data most plausible.
  • Method of moments — choose $\theta$ so the model's first moment (its mean) equals the data's first moment.

For the Gamma family with known shape these two coincide. They do not coincide in general — for many models the MLE and the moment estimator are different numbers — so it is worth one sentence in the notebook noting the agreement rather than assuming it always happens.

Free sanity check If you ever compute $\hat\theta$ and find $2\hat\theta \ne \bar t$, you have made an arithmetic error. This identity is a zero-cost check you can run in your head.

How sure are we? Not very — n is 5

"$\hat\theta = 10.6$ TB" is a single number, and a single number pretends to a precision you do not have. Five observations is a very small sample. The honest question is: how wide is the range of $\theta$ values that would also have explained this data well?

The curvature line from the sub-chapter answers it. The Fisher information is exactly minus the second derivative you already computed:

$$I(\hat\theta) = \frac{2n}{\hat\theta^{2}} = \frac{10}{10.6^{2}} = 0.0890 \qquad \mathrm{se}(\hat\theta) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{I(\hat\theta)}} = 3.352 \;\text{TB}$$ $$\text{approximate 95\% interval: } \hat\theta \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{se} = [\,4.03,\; 17.17\,]\ \text{TB}$$

Read that band. The upper end is more than four times the lower end. Translated into the quantity the business cares about, $E[T] = 2\theta$, the data are consistent with an expected system lifetime anywhere from about 8 TB to about 34 TB. That is the difference between "replace these quarterly" and "replace these annually".

Two caveats to state, not hide (1) This interval uses the asymptotic normal approximation for the MLE, which is justified for large $n$. With $n = 5$ it is indicative, not exact — the true sampling distribution of $\hat\theta$ is right-skewed, so an exact or bootstrap interval would be shifted upward. (2) The lower bound stays comfortably above zero, so the approximation is not producing anything impossible here; that is worth checking, because for small samples it sometimes does.

Sanity check: does the shape-2 model fit?

Fitting $\theta$ only tunes the horizontal stretch. It cannot tell you whether the shape was right in the first place — the arithmetic would happily hand you a $\hat\theta$ even if the data came from something else entirely. So check the shape separately, using a quantity that does not depend on $\theta$ at all.

That quantity is the coefficient of variation (standard deviation ÷ mean). Because $\theta$ scales the mean and the standard deviation by the same factor, it cancels out of the ratio — leaving a fingerprint of the shape alone:

model: $\;\mathrm{CV} = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}\,\theta}{2\theta} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = 0.707$   (for any $\theta$ whatsoever)
your data: $\;\mathrm{CV} = \dfrac{16.50}{21.2} = 0.778$

0.778 against a target of 0.707 is a gap of about 10%, on a statistic computed from five observations. With $n = 5$ the sample coefficient of variation is itself very noisy, so this is well inside what chance produces. Verdict: the shape-2 model is plausible. Note also that the sample value sits clearly below 1.0, where a single exponential would be — so the data do support the "two routers in sequence" structure rather than one.

In the notebook Report it as a qualitative check, not a test: "the observed coefficient of variation, 0.778, is close to the 0.707 implied by $\Gamma(2,\theta)$, so the shape assumption is not contradicted by the data." Resist upgrading this to a formal goodness-of-fit test — with $n = 5$, any such test has almost no power and quoting a p-value would overstate what you know.

Watch out! Five traps

  1. Fitting $f_S$ to $T$-data. Your five numbers are dual-system totals, so the model is $\Gamma(2,\theta)$. Reaching for the single-router exponential by reflex gives $\hat\theta = \bar t = 21.2$ — exactly double the right answer, and silently wrong because nothing crashes.
  2. Scale versus rate. Our $\theta$ multiplies (a scale): mean $= 2\theta$. Many textbooks and libraries write the exponential and Gamma with a rate $\lambda = 1/\theta$ that divides. In SciPy the call is gamma(a=2, scale=theta) — pass a rate where a scale belongs and your answer is inverted. Task 6 hits this same trap much harder; it is worth internalising here.
  3. Confusing the mode with the mean. For $T$ the peak is at $\theta = 10.6$ but the average is $2\theta = 21.2$. Right-skewed distributions always separate these two. Say which one you are quoting.
  4. Reporting $\hat\theta$ with no uncertainty. With $n = 5$ the plausible range is roughly $[4.0, 17.2]$. A point estimate alone is technically correct and practically misleading.
  5. Units and branch. $\xi_{10}$ is in terabytes, so $\hat\theta$, $E[T]$ and every axis label are in TB. And state explicitly that $\xi_9 = 0$ selects the exponential branch, and that the other two branches are not applicable to your paper.

Quiz

Q1. A classmate fits the single-router density to the five numbers and reports $\hat\theta = 21.2$ TB. What went wrong, and why is the error exactly a factor of two?

Reveal answer

They used $f_S$ (shape 1) instead of $f_T$ (shape 2). The scale estimate is always $\bar t$ divided by the shape, so halving the shape from 2 to 1 doubles the estimate: $21.2/1$ instead of the correct $21.2/2 = 10.6$.

Q2. The exam asks for "a transformation whose maximum is the same". Why does taking the logarithm guarantee the location of the maximum does not move?

Reveal answer

Because $\ln$ is strictly increasing: if $\mathcal{L}(\theta_1) > \mathcal{L}(\theta_2)$ then $\ln\mathcal{L}(\theta_1) > \ln\mathcal{L}(\theta_2)$. Every pairwise comparison between candidates is preserved, so the winner is preserved. Only the heights change, never the ranking.

Q3. In the convolution, why did the factor $e^{-u/\theta}\,e^{-(t-u)/\theta}$ collapse to something with no $u$ in it — and what would have gone wrong if the two routers had different scales $\theta_1 \ne \theta_2$?

Reveal answer

Adding the exponents gives $-u/\theta - (t-u)/\theta = -t/\theta$, so the $u$ cancels and the integrand becomes constant. With different scales the exponent is $-u/\theta_1 - (t-u)/\theta_2$, which still depends on $u$; the integral then produces a difference of two exponentials, and the answer is not a Gamma density. Equal scales is a load-bearing assumption, not a convenience.

Q4. Your model says $E[T] = 21.2$ TB and the average of your data is also 21.2 TB. Is that a sign you have accidentally reasoned in a circle?

Reveal answer

No. Nothing forced the model mean to match the sample mean; it fell out of solving $\ell'(\theta) = 0$, which for this family happens to reduce to exactly that condition. It is a property of the Gamma family with known shape (where maximum likelihood and the method of moments coincide), not an assumption smuggled in.

Q5. Without any calculation: would a sample of 50 systems have produced a narrower interval for $\theta$, and by roughly how much?

Reveal answer

Yes. The standard error is $\hat\theta/\sqrt{2n}$, so it shrinks with the square root of the sample size. Going from $n=5$ to $n=50$ multiplies $n$ by 10 and therefore divides the width by $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.2$ — the interval would be roughly a third as wide, assuming $\hat\theta$ itself came out similar.
