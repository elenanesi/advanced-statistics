Bayes in one picture

Before symbols: a Bayesian analysis has three characters, and the whole method is "multiply the first two, look at the third".

$$\underbrace{f(\theta \mid x)}_{\text{posterior}} \;\propto\; \underbrace{f(x \mid \theta)}_{\text{likelihood}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{f(\theta)}_{\text{prior}}$$ the ∝ ("proportional to") hides a normalising constant that does not depend on θ — you recover it at the end by recognising the family

That "recognise the family" step is the entire mechanical trick of this task, and it has a name: conjugacy. Because the prior and the likelihood have compatible algebraic shapes, the product is a Gamma again and you never integrate anything.

⚠ The trap: is that number a rate or a scale?

Bayes: Someone tells you "the pace is 6". Six minutes per kilometre, or six kilometres per minute? Same information, reciprocal numbers, wildly different runner. The Gamma's second parameter has exactly this problem.

The Gamma distribution is written two different ways in the literature, and both use a Greek letter that looks the same. Here they are side by side:

The one rule that resolves it Look at where the parameter appears in the exponent, not at its name. If it is dividing $x$, it is a scale. If it is multiplying $x$, it is a rate. Then apply your reading consistently to every Gamma in the problem — the mistake that costs marks is flipping the convention halfway through.
SUB-CHAPTER

Reading Hogg's Gamma, symbol by symbol

This exam sits on Hogg, McKean & Craig, so their convention is the one that governs. They write the Gamma density as:

$$f(x) \;=\; \frac{x^{\alpha-1}\,e^{-x/\beta}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\,\beta^{\alpha}}, \qquad x > 0$$ look at the exponent: $-x/\beta$. β is dividing x.

Two independent confirmations that $\beta$ is a scale in this book, either of which you can quote in the notebook:

  1. The exponent. $e^{-x/\beta}$ divides, so $\beta$ carries the same units as $x$ and stretches the axis. That is the definition of a scale parameter.
  2. The normaliser. The denominator contains $\beta^{\alpha}$, which is what you need to cancel the $\beta^{\alpha}$ produced by integrating $x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x/\beta}$. Under the rate convention the constant would sit in the numerator as $\lambda^{\alpha}$ instead. So the position of $\beta$ in the fraction is itself a tell.
SourceSecond parameterMean
Hogg, McKean & Craig (this course)$\beta$ = scale$\alpha\beta$
SciPy gamma(a, scale=...)scale = scale$a \cdot$ scale
Most Bayesian texts, PyMC, R's rgamma(rate=)$\beta$ = rate$\alpha/\beta$

So SciPy happens to agree with Hogg, and much of the Bayesian literature does not. If your notebook mixes a Hogg derivation with a formula copied from a Bayesian blog post, this is exactly where the silent error enters.

Practical habit Never write "$\mathrm{Gamma}(23, 63)$" on its own in the workbook. Write "$\mathrm{Gamma}(\text{shape } 23,\ \text{scale } 63)$" or "$\mathrm{Gamma}(\text{shape } 53,\ \text{rate } 954.815873)$". It costs six words and removes the ambiguity permanently — and the exam guidelines explicitly ask you to explain any notation you use.

Applying the convention to THIS task

Two Gammas appear in this problem, and the trap is that they need different-looking treatment while following the same rule. Take them one at a time.

The data: Gamma with α = 3 and β = 1/θ

Under Hogg, $\beta$ is the scale — so the scale is $1/\theta$. Substituting into the density, the exponent becomes $-x/(1/\theta) = -\theta x$, with $\theta$ now multiplying $x$:

$$f(x \mid \theta) \;=\; \frac{x^{2}\,e^{-x/(1/\theta)}}{\Gamma(3)\,(1/\theta)^{3}} \;=\; \frac{\theta^{3}\,x^{2}\,e^{-\theta x}}{\Gamma(3)}, \qquad x > 0$$ so θ is a RATE for x — that flip is deliberate on the exam's part, and it is the reason $E[x] = 3/\theta$

The prior: θ ~ Gamma with α = 23 and β = 63

Same rule, applied again: $\beta = 63$ is a scale, so the exponent is $-\theta/63$ and the prior's rate is the reciprocal:

$$f(\theta) \;\propto\; \theta^{\,23-1}\,e^{-\theta/63} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{prior rate} \;=\; \frac{1}{63} \;=\; 0.015873$$
Where people go wrong — the exact misstep They notice that $\theta$ turned out to be a rate for the data, and then assume "so everything in this problem is in rate form", and treat the prior's 63 as a rate too. That is not consistency, it is contagion. The consistent move is to read every printed β as a scale. For the data that happens to make $\theta$ a rate (because the printed scale is $1/\theta$); for the prior it makes 63 a scale, full stop. Slide 12 prices this error at 6.19%.

Both densities are now in the same currency — everything is written in terms of $\theta$ with an exponent of the form $e^{-\theta \times (\text{something})}$ — and that is what makes the next two slides pure bookkeeping.

The likelihood: what the ten observations say

Ten independent observations, so the joint density is the product of the ten individual ones. Throw away every factor that does not contain $\theta$ (the $\propto$ sign licenses that):

$$f(x_1,\ldots,x_{10} \mid \theta) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{10} \frac{\theta^{3}x_i^{2}e^{-\theta x_i}}{\Gamma(3)} \;\propto\; \theta^{3n}\,\exp\!\Big(-\theta\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i\Big)$$ with $n = 10$:   $\theta^{30}\,e^{-\theta \sum x_i}$

This is why the exam only gave you the mean. The only thing the data contribute is the pair (count of observations, total of observations) — and the total is recoverable from the mean:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{10} x_i \;=\; n\bar x \;=\; 10 \times 95.48 \;=\; 954.8$$ the individual values are irrelevant; $(n, \sum x_i)$ is a sufficient statistic

Read the two exponents as two separate ledgers, because this reading makes the posterior obvious:

  • The power of $\theta$ — here $30$ — is a count ledger: how many observations' worth of evidence you hold.
  • The number multiplying $\theta$ in the exponential — here $954.8$ — is an exposure ledger: how much total magnitude those observations came to.

A Gamma prior is written in exactly the same two-ledger form. Adding beliefs to data will therefore be addition, twice.

The posterior: two additions, no integrals

Multiply the two kernels and collect powers. Nothing here is harder than adding exponents:

$$f(\theta \mid x) \;\propto\; \underbrace{\theta^{30}e^{-\theta(954.8)}}_{\text{likelihood}} \cdot \underbrace{\theta^{22}e^{-\theta(1/63)}}_{\text{prior}} \;=\; \theta^{\,30+22}\;e^{-\theta\left(954.8 + \frac{1}{63}\right)}$$ $$=\; \theta^{\,53-1}\;e^{-\theta\,(954.815873)}$$

That is a Gamma kernel, so the posterior is a Gamma — no integration was ever needed. In shape–rate language:

$$\boxed{\;\theta \mid x \;\sim\; \mathrm{Gamma}\big(\text{shape } 53,\ \text{rate } 954.815873\big)\;}$$ equivalently, in Hogg's shape–scale form:   $\mathrm{Gamma}\big(\text{shape } 53,\ \text{scale } 0.00104732\big)$

(b) Squared-error loss → the posterior mean

The posterior is a whole distribution, but a decision-maker wants one number. Which number is "best" is not a mathematical fact — it depends on how you get punished for being wrong. That punishment is the loss function.

squared-error loss:   $L(\theta, a) = (\theta - a)^{2}$   being off by 2 hurts four times as much as being off by 1

Under this loss the best single number is the posterior mean — always, for any posterior. The proof is two lines, so it belongs in the notebook:

Why squared-error loss picks the mean (two lines)

Write $m = E[\theta \mid x]$ and expand the expected loss around it:

$$E\big[(\theta - a)^{2} \mid x\big] \;=\; E\big[(\theta - m)^{2} \mid x\big] \;+\; (m - a)^{2}$$

The cross term vanishes because $E[\theta - m \mid x] = 0$ by definition of $m$. The first term is the posterior variance, which you cannot control. The second is a square, so it is minimised — at zero — by choosing $a = m$. Hence the Bayes estimate under squared-error loss is the posterior mean, and the residual risk equals the posterior variance.

For a Gamma in shape–rate form the mean is shape ÷ rate:

$$\hat\theta_{\text{Bayes}} \;=\; E[\theta \mid x] \;=\; \frac{53}{954.815873} \;=\; \boxed{0.0555081}$$
Do not skip the justification Part (b) says "under squared-error loss" for a reason: the grader wants to see that you know the loss function is what selects the mean, not that the mean is universally "the answer". Under absolute-error loss the Bayes estimate would be the posterior median; under 0–1 loss it would be the mode — which is exactly what part (c) asks for next.

(c) The posterior mode

The mode is the peak: the single most plausible value of $\theta$ after seeing the data. Find it by maximising the log of the posterior kernel, which is the same calculus move as Task 3:

$$\ln f(\theta \mid x) = \text{const} + (a-1)\ln\theta - b\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{a-1}{\theta} - b = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta_{\text{mode}} = \frac{a-1}{b}$$ valid whenever the shape $a > 1$; here $a = 53$, so we are safe
$$\hat\theta_{\text{mode}} \;=\; \frac{53 - 1}{954.815873} \;=\; \frac{52}{954.815873} \;=\; \boxed{0.0544608}$$

Mean is above mode, and it always will be here. The Gamma is right-skewed: it has a hard floor at zero and an unbounded tail to the right, so the balance point of the mass sits to the right of the peak. Algebraically, mean − mode $= \frac{a}{b} - \frac{a-1}{b} = \frac{1}{b}$, which for you is $1/954.815873 = 0.00105$ — exactly the gap you see.

Free check Mean minus mode should equal one over the posterior rate. If your two answers do not differ by exactly $1/b$, one of them is wrong.

How much did the prior actually move things?

Compare against the estimate you would have got with no prior at all. From Task 3's machinery: for a Gamma with known shape $\alpha$ and rate $\theta$, the maximum likelihood estimate is $\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \alpha/\bar x$:

$$\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{3}{95.48} = 0.0314202 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad \hat\theta_{\text{Bayes}} = 0.0555081$$ the posterior mean is 77% higher than the data-only estimate

That is a big move, and the two-ledger reading from slide 6 explains exactly where it came from:

The mean is shape ÷ rate. The prior inflates the numerator by 77% and leaves the denominator essentially untouched, so the estimate is pushed up by 77%. The prior is acting almost entirely through the shape ledger.

The 95% credible interval is $[0.0416,\ 0.0714]$, and the data-only estimate 0.0314 sits outside it. That is a striking, concrete illustration of prior influence: after updating, the analysis assigns less than 2.5% belief to the value that the data alone would have chosen. Whether that is acceptable depends entirely on whether you actually believe the prior.

A prior in the wrong postcode — that still matters

Now look at where the prior actually lives. Its mean is shape × scale:

prior mean $= 23 \times 63 = 1449$      posterior mean $= 0.0555081$

Those are four orders of magnitude apart. On a logarithmic axis the distance is almost comic:

So the prior is badly calibrated to this data — and yet it changed the answer by 77%. That is not a contradiction, and understanding why is worth a paragraph in the workbook.

Why a far-away prior can still bite A Gamma prior behaves like a pseudo-sample: its shape contributes counts and its rate contributes exposure. This prior supplies 23 counts but essentially zero exposure (0.0159 against the data's 954.8). It is as if someone handed you seven or eight extra observations and then refused to tell you how large they were. The count survives into the numerator, the missing exposure never reaches the denominator, and the ratio is dragged upward. A prior that was merely wide but internally consistent would have been far more harmless than this one.

Write this in the notebook as a limitation, not a defect to hide. The exam prescribes the prior; your job is to use it correctly and to say honestly what it is doing. A sentence noting that the result is prior-sensitive, plus the MLE as a reference point, is exactly the kind of judgement the workbook guidelines ask for.

The price of not declaring your convention

Suppose you had read $\xi_{18} = 63$ as a rate instead of a scale. Nothing would have looked wrong. The arithmetic is just as clean, the posterior is still a Gamma, and no error message appears anywhere. You simply add 63 to the exposure ledger instead of $1/63$:

What to actually do in the workbook Solve it with the scale reading, because that is Hogg's convention and Hogg is the prescribed source. Then add one short remark: state that $\xi_{18}$ was interpreted as a scale following Hogg's density, give the alternative numbers in a footnote or a single sentence, and note the 6.19% difference. This turns a hidden ambiguity into a demonstration that you spotted it — which is worth more than either answer on its own.

Watch out! Five traps

  1. Flipping the convention halfway. Read every printed $\beta$ as a scale. For the data that makes $\theta$ a rate; for the prior it makes 63 a scale. Both statements come from the same rule applied twice — resist the urge to "keep everything in rate form".
  2. Adding 63 to 954.8. The single most likely arithmetic slip in this task. The number that joins the exposure ledger is $1/63 = 0.015873$, not 63.
  3. Reporting the mode for part (b). Squared-error loss selects the mean. The mode is part (c). They differ by $1/b$ and marks are lost for swapping them.
  4. Shape $-1$ in the wrong place. Mean $= a/b$ uses the shape; mode $= (a-1)/b$ subtracts one. Also state the condition $a > 1$, without which the mode is at zero and the formula is meaningless.
  5. Presenting the posterior mean without context. It sits 77% above the data-only estimate and outside a credible interval built from the same posterior. Report the MLE alongside it and say one honest sentence about prior sensitivity.

Quiz

Q1. The data density is Gamma with $\beta = 1/\theta$. Is $\theta$ a rate or a scale for $x$ — and how do you know without looking anything up?

Reveal answer

A rate. Substitute $\beta = 1/\theta$ into Hogg's exponent $-x/\beta$ and you get $-\theta x$: $\theta$ is now multiplying $x$, which is the definition of a rate. Confirmation from the mean: $E[x] = \alpha\beta = 3/\theta$, shape divided by $\theta$ — the rate formula.

Q2. Under squared-error loss, do you report the posterior mean or the posterior mode — and why is the answer not simply "whichever is bigger"?

Reveal answer

The mean. Expanding $E[(\theta-a)^2 \mid x]$ gives posterior variance plus $(m-a)^2$, so the expected loss is minimised at $a = m = E[\theta \mid x]$. The choice is driven by the loss function, not by the size of the numbers: a different loss would select a different summary of the very same posterior (absolute-error → median, 0–1 → mode).

Q3. Why did the exam hand you only $\bar x$ and not the ten individual observations?

Reveal answer

Because for a Gamma with known shape, the sum $\sum x_i$ is a sufficient statistic for $\theta$ — the likelihood depends on the data only through $n$ and $\sum x_i$. The mean gives you the sum immediately ($10 \times 95.48 = 954.8$), and the individual values would add nothing.

Q4. The posterior mean is 0.0555 but the prior mean is 1449. If the prior is centred so far from the answer, how can it be influencing the result at all?

Reveal answer

Because the prior's two contributions travel through different channels. Its shape adds 23 to a numerator of 53 — nearly half. Its rate adds only 0.0159 to a denominator of 954.82 — essentially nothing. The prior's location (its mean) never enters the update directly; only the two ledgers do, and one of them is heavily loaded. A prior can therefore be badly centred and still move the answer a lot.

Q5. A classmate reports the posterior as Gamma(53, 1017.8). What did they do, and how would you spot it in thirty seconds?

Reveal answer

They read $\xi_{18} = 63$ as a rate and added 63 to the exposure ledger. Spot it by checking the magnitude: $63$ against $1/63$ is a factor of about 4000, so the rate ledger should barely move from 954.8. Anything that shifts it by tens of units means the reciprocal was skipped. Their point estimates come out 6.19% low.

Tool trust — the checks that make a grader relax

  • scipy.stats.gamma(a=53, scale=1/954.815873) — SciPy takes a scale, so pass the reciprocal of the rate. Say so in a comment; this is the same trap in code form.
  • Confirm .mean() returns 0.0555081 and that .pdf is maximised at 0.0544608 — analytic and library values agreeing is the cheapest credibility you can buy.
  • Verify mean − mode $= 1/954.815873 = 0.00104732$ exactly.
  • Get the credible interval from .ppf([0.025, 0.975]) and check that 0.0314202 falls outside it, as the interpretation slide claims.
  • Optional but persuasive: recompute the posterior by brute-force numerical multiplication of prior × likelihood on a grid, normalise, and show it lands on the same curve. That demonstrates the conjugacy result rather than asserting it.
