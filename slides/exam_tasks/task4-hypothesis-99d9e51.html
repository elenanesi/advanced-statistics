TASK 4: DID THE WEIGHTS GO UP?

Forge: The old production line made hammers averaging 842 g over 1000 pieces. New system, 10 new hammers. Your branch (ξ₁₃=2) asks: is the mean now higher? Look at the spread below before you guess.

Parameter signature 99d9e51. Book: Unit 3.2 (Normal) + Unit 7.1–7.2 (hypothesis testing).

The two averages are almost on top of each other, and the ten dots are scattered over 360 g. That picture is the whole answer — the rest of this deck makes it rigorous.

The question, in plain words

A factory replaced its hammer-making system. Somebody has to sign a sentence like "the new system produces heavier hammers" — and be responsible for it.

What we are given

SymbolValueWhere it comes from
$\mu_0$ (ξ₁₁)842 gMean of a long run of 1000 hammers on the old system
$\sigma_0$ (ξ₁₂)55.3 gStandard deviation of that same long run
ξ₁₄10 weights719, 743, 803, 814, 925, 1051, 776, 806, 802, 1083
The 1000-hammer history is what makes this a z-test rather than a t-test. Hold that thought — it is the trap on slide 8.

Branch ξ₁₃=2 fixes the direction of the question: higher, not "different", not "lower", not "more variable".

The model: a bell curve of weights

Picture the factory running all day. Most hammers come out near the target; a few come out light, a few heavy, and the further you go from the middle the rarer they get. That shape — one hump, symmetric, thin tails — is what a Normal distribution looks like.

$W \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,\ \sigma^{2})$

Read that as: "the weight $W$ of one randomly chosen hammer is Normal with unknown centre $\mu$ and spread $\sigma$." The centre $\mu$ is the thing the business cares about; it is what the test is about.

Why we need a model at all

Without a model, "852.2 is bigger than 842" is just arithmetic. The model is what lets us say how surprising that gap is when nothing has actually changed. No model, no probability, no decision.

Careful with symbols: $\mu$ is the true, unknown mean of the new process (a fixed number we never see). $\bar x$ is the average of our 10 hammers (a number we computed, which would change if we grabbed 10 different hammers).

Assumptions — and the shaky one

Write these four in the workbook. A test is only as trustworthy as the assumptions behind it.

  1. Independence. One hammer's weight tells you nothing about the next one's. Breaks if hammers come in batches that share a bad steel delivery.
  2. Identical process. All 10 come from the same new system, settings unchanged during sampling.
  3. Normality. Weights are roughly bell-shaped. With $n=10$ this matters; the Central Limit Theorem is not a rescue at this size.
  4. $\sigma$ known and unchanged at 55.3 g, carried over from the old 1000-hammer run.
Assumption 4 is the uncertain one. The new system changed the machine — why would it change the mean but leave the spread untouched? We will come back to this on slide 16 with a number that makes it look genuinely doubtful.
$\mu_0=\xi_{11}=842\ \text{g},\qquad \sigma_0=\xi_{12}=55.3\ \text{g},\qquad n=10$

What a hypothesis actually is

Think of a courtroom. The defendant is presumed innocent. The prosecution must produce evidence strong enough that innocence becomes implausible. If it fails, the verdict is "not guilty" — which is not the same as "proven innocent".

CourtroomStatisticsHere
Presumption of innocenceNull hypothesis $H_0$The new system did not raise the mean
Prosecution's claimAlternative $H_1$The new system raised the mean
EvidenceThe sample10 weights, average 852.2 g
"Beyond reasonable doubt"Significance level $\alpha$5%

The asymmetry is deliberate. $H_0$ is the boring, cheap-to-be-wrong-about claim; $H_1$ is the expensive claim that would trigger action. We only abandon $H_0$ when the data would be genuinely weird if $H_0$ were true.

Why "higher" forces one tail

$H_0:\ \mu \le 842\ \text{g}\qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_1:\ \mu > 842\ \text{g}$

The question is directional — "does the new system make higher weights?" So only one kind of result counts as evidence: a sample mean far above 842. A sample mean far below 842 is not weak evidence for $H_1$, it is evidence against it.

Spending the whole 5% on one tail lowers the bar from 1.96 to 1.645, so the one-sided test is the more powerful choice for this question. Choosing the direction after seeing the data would be cheating; here the direction comes from the branch, before any data.

Why write $H_0:\mu\le 842$ rather than $H_0:\mu=842$?

Logically the two hypotheses should cover everything, and "not higher" means "$\le$". Operationally it makes no difference: the hardest case for us to detect inside $H_0$ is $\mu=842$ exactly, so that is the value we compute with. Both notations are accepted; state which you use.

Turning grams into "how surprising"

Is a 10.2 g gap big? Impossible to say in grams. It only becomes meaningful when measured in units of how much the sample mean naturally wobbles.

$\text{standard error}=\dfrac{\sigma_0}{\sqrt n}=\dfrac{55.3}{\sqrt{10}}=17.4874\ \text{g}$

Even if the new system were identical to the old one, the average of 10 hammers would bounce around 842 by roughly ±17 g just from luck of the draw. Our observed gap is 10.2 g — less than one unit of natural wobble.

$z=\dfrac{\bar x-\mu_0}{\sigma_0/\sqrt n}\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{under }H_0\ }\ \mathcal{N}(0,1)$
Lookalike alert — standard deviation vs standard error.
$\sigma=55.3$ g describes how much one hammer varies. $\sigma/\sqrt n=17.49$ g describes how much the average of ten varies. Averaging cancels noise, so the average is always the calmer quantity — by a factor $\sqrt n$. Using 55.3 in the denominator is one of the most common exam mistakes.

z or t? The one question that decides it

Both statistics have the identical shape "(observed gap) ÷ (standard error)". They differ in one thing: where the number in the denominator came from.

Never let the choice of ruler be implicit. In the workbook: state that $\sigma$ is treated as known from the 1000-hammer run, report the z-test as the main result, then report the t-statistic as a robustness check. That is exactly the "flag the uncertain assumption" move the graders are looking for.

The decision rule, as a logical statement

Fix the rule before looking at the number. That is what makes 5% mean 5%.

Reject $H_0$ $\iff$ $z > z_{0.95}=1.644854$

Because $z$ is just a rescaled $\bar x$, the same rule can be written in grams — which is much easier to explain to a factory manager:

$\bar x > \mu_0 + z_{0.95}\cdot\dfrac{\sigma_0}{\sqrt n} = 842 + 1.644854\times 17.4874 = \mathbf{870.76\ g}$
So the whole test collapses to one sentence: "the ten hammers would have had to average more than 870.76 g." They averaged 852.2 g. That is 18.6 g short of the bar.

The set of outcomes that trigger rejection is the rejection region: here $\{\bar x > 870.76\}$, equivalently $\{z > 1.644854\}$.

The picture: nowhere near the line

The teal line is our result. It sits inside the crowd of perfectly ordinary outcomes, well to the left of the crimson cutoff.

What the p-value is — and is not

$p=0.279853$ means: if the new system had changed nothing, we would see a sample mean at least this high about 28% of the time, purely by sampling luck. Roughly one run in four. Nothing remarkable happened.

It is not the probability that $H_0$ is true, and not the probability that the new system failed. It is a probability computed assuming $H_0$ — about the data, not about the hypothesis.

Conclusion: fail to reject

$z=0.583277 \;<\; 1.644854 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \textbf{do not reject } H_0$
Verdict to write in the workbook. At the 5% significance level there is no evidence that the new system produces hammers with a higher mean weight. The observed increase of 10.2 g is well inside what sampling variation alone produces when $\sigma_0=55.3$ g and $n=10$ (equivalently, $p=0.2799$; equivalently, $\bar x=852.2$ g fell short of the 870.76 g bar).

The discipline: "fail to reject" ≠ "accept"

Do not writeWrite
"We accept $H_0$.""We fail to reject $H_0$."
"The new system does not raise weight.""The data do not provide evidence, at the 5% level, that it raises weight."
"There is no effect.""Any effect is too small for this sample to detect."

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. A test can fail for two very different reasons: nothing changed, or something changed but our measuring instrument was too blunt to see it. The next three slides show that here it is largely the second.

Two ways to be wrong

The task explicitly asks for the error probabilities. There are exactly two ways a test can be wrong, and they are not symmetric.

Truth: $\mu\le 842$ (no lift)Truth: $\mu > 842$ (real lift)
We reject $H_0$Type I error — false alarm. Probability $\alpha$Correct detection. Probability $1-\beta$ = power
We fail to rejectCorrect. Probability $1-\alpha$Type II error — missed a real lift. Probability $\beta$

Type I: fixed by us, at 5%

$\mathbb{P}(\text{Type I})=\mathbb{P}(z > 1.644854 \mid \mu=842)=\alpha=0.05$

This one needs no data. We chose it when we picked the cutoff: 5% of the time, an unchanged process would hand us a sample mean above 870.76 g and we would raise a false alarm. Type I error probability is a design decision, not a measurement.

Type II is different. $\beta$ is not a single number — it depends on how big the real increase is. A 200 g increase is nearly impossible to miss; a 1 g increase is nearly impossible to catch. So $\beta$ has to be quoted at a stated value of $\mu$.

Power here: only 14.4%

Take the observed mean 852.2 g at face value as the true one, and ask: with $n=10$, how often would this test actually catch an increase of that size?

$\text{power}(\mu)=1-\Phi\!\left(z_{0.95}-\frac{\mu-\mu_0}{\sigma_0/\sqrt n}\right),\qquad \text{power}(852.2)=1-\Phi(1.644854-0.583277)=\mathbf{0.1442}$

Read that last row slowly. If the lift were real and exactly this size, this test would miss it 6 times out of 7. Failing to reject was the overwhelmingly likely outcome regardless of the truth — so the non-rejection carries very little information.

n = 10 is simply too small

Flip the question round. Instead of "did we detect it?", ask: how big would the increase have to be for this design to have a decent chance (the conventional 80%) of catching it?

$\delta_{80\%}=\left(z_{0.95}+z_{0.80}\right)\dfrac{\sigma_0}{\sqrt n}=(1.644854+0.841621)\times 17.4874=\mathbf{43.48\ \text{g}}$
43.48 g needed, 10.2 g observed. The instrument is calibrated to detect a change four times larger than the one in front of us. A non-rejection from a design like this says more about the sample size than about the hammers.

Because $\delta_{80\%}$ shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$, detecting a genuine 10.2 g lift with 80% power would need roughly $n=(2.4865\times55.3/10.2)^2\approx 182$ hammers. That is the constructive recommendation to put in the workbook: this is not a verdict on the new system, it is a request for more data.

Red flag: the spread more than doubled

We assumed $\sigma$ was still 55.3 g. The sample disagrees, loudly.

$s=125.653\ \text{g},\qquad \dfrac{s}{\sigma_0}=\dfrac{125.653}{55.3}=2.272,\qquad \dfrac{s^{2}}{\sigma_0^{2}}=\mathbf{5.16}$

The observed variance is 5.16 times the historical variance. Some inflation is expected — with only 9 degrees of freedom the sample variance is a jumpy estimate — but a factor of five is a lot, and two hammers sit more than 4 historical standard deviations above $\mu_0$.

The honest critical remark. If the new system really did inflate the spread, then $\sigma_0=55.3$ understates the true standard error, the z-statistic is too large, and the real evidence is even weaker than 0.583 suggests — consistent with $t=0.257$. Either way the conclusion holds, but the interesting managerial finding may not be the mean at all: the new system looks less consistent. That is worth flagging even though branch ξ₁₃=2 does not ask about variance.

Quest complete

Forge: The exciting answer would have been "yes, heavier". The honest answer is "we cannot tell with ten hammers" — and saying that clearly is the harder skill.
  • Model $W\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$; four assumptions stated, $\sigma$ known flagged as the shaky one
  • One-sided $H_0:\mu\le842$ vs $H_1:\mu>842$ — direction comes from branch ξ₁₃=2, not from the data
  • Rule fixed in advance: reject iff $z>1.644854$, i.e. $\bar x>870.76$ g
  • $\bar x=852.2$, $z=0.583277$, $p=0.279853$ → fail to reject; no evidence of higher weights at 5%
  • $\sigma$ known → z; $\sigma$ from the sample → t. Robustness: $t_9=0.256701$, $p=0.401591$, same verdict

Quick self-check before the notebook

Q1. A colleague writes "p = 0.28, so there is a 28% chance the new system is no better." What is wrong?
The p-value is computed assuming $H_0$ is true; it is the probability of data this extreme, not the probability of a hypothesis.

Q2. Same data, but suppose the task had never mentioned the 1000-hammer history. Which test, and does the verdict change?
t-test on 9 degrees of freedom: $t=0.256701$ against 1.833113, $p=0.401591$. Same verdict, weaker-looking statistic.

Q3. Why is reporting $\beta=85.58\%$ more useful to the factory than reporting "not significant"?
It converts a null result into an actionable statement: the study was too small, run about 180 hammers to detect a 10 g lift.
