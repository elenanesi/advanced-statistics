TASK 5: DEGREE-10 OLS + RIDGE

Ridge: 22 points, 11 coefficients, and $y$ values ranging from −0.98 to −6×10¹³. The statistics here is easy. The arithmetic is what will bite you.

Branch ξ₁₅=2 → polynomial of degree 10, so 11 coefficients $\alpha_0,\dots,\alpha_{10}$. Parameter signature 99d9e51. Book: Unit 6.2 (OLS) + 6.4 (ridge).

Everything in this deck follows from that picture: on a linear scale, most of the dataset is indistinguishable from zero.

What the task actually asks

  1. Fit a polynomial of degree 10 to the 22 given points by ordinary least squares (OLS — the classic "minimise the sum of squared vertical distances" fit).
  2. Fit the same model by ridge regression (least squares plus a penalty on the size of the coefficients).
  3. Justify the penalty weight $\lambda$ — not just pick one.
  4. Discuss the qualities of each solution. This is the part that earns marks, and slide 16 is the honest answer to it.

Why this matters so much. Least squares minimises absolute squared error. One unit of error at $x=20$ costs the same as one unit of error at $x=0$ — but the values at $x=20$ are $10^{13}$ times larger. So the fit will be decided almost entirely by the six or so points at the edges, and it will treat everything in the middle as "close enough to zero". Keep this in mind: it comes back as the punchline on slide 16.

The key insight: this is linear regression

"Degree-10 polynomial" sounds like a nonlinear problem. It is not — and seeing why is the single most useful idea in this task.

$y \approx \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 x + \alpha_2 x^{2} + \cdots + \alpha_{10}x^{10}$

The curve is bendy in $x$, yes. But look at how the unknowns enter: every $\alpha_j$ is multiplied by something and added. No $\alpha^2$, no $\sin(\alpha)$, no $\alpha_1\alpha_2$. That is the definition of linear in the parameters — which is all that ordinary least squares requires.

The trick: treat $x, x^2, \ldots, x^{10}$ as ten separate predictor columns that you happened to compute from one variable. Then this is just multiple linear regression with 10 predictors plus an intercept, and every standard formula applies unchanged.

Analogy: a recipe can call for flour, flour², and flour³ as if they were three different ingredients. Once they are in separate jars, the cook does not care where they came from — they just weigh them and mix.

Terminology to keep straight: linear in the parameters (what OLS needs — satisfied here) is not the same as a straight line in $x$ (visibly false here). The exam loves this distinction.

The design matrix

Stack one row per data point, one column per power. That gives the 22×11 Vandermonde design matrix:

$X=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & x_1 & x_1^{2} & \cdots & x_1^{10}\\ 1 & x_2 & x_2^{2} & \cdots & x_2^{10}\\ \vdots & & & & \vdots\\ 1 & x_{22} & x_{22}^{2} & \cdots & x_{22}^{10}\end{pmatrix},\qquad y=X\alpha+\varepsilon$

Two concrete rows, so the problem is visible rather than abstract:

Look at the last column against the first. Inside a single matrix we have entries of size 1 and entries of size $10^{13}$. That is not a cosmetic annoyance — the next two slides show it destroys most of the precision available in double-precision arithmetic.

Free gift: $x=0$ is in the sample

Substitute $x=0$ into the polynomial. Every power term vanishes:

$y(0)=\alpha_0+\alpha_1\cdot 0+\alpha_2\cdot 0^{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{10}\cdot 0^{10}=\alpha_0$

And the dataset contains exactly that point: $(0,\ -0.98)$. So the true intercept is, up to observation noise, $\alpha_0 \approx -0.98$ — read straight off the data, with no fitting at all.

Use this as a diagnostic, not as a constraint. Fit normally, then compare the fitted intercept against −0.98. If a fit returns an intercept of, say, $+1.9\times10^{11}$, you have learned something devastating about that fit without any further analysis. (Spoiler: OLS returns exactly that. Slide 16.)

Anchors like this are cheap and worth hunting for in any regression task: a point where most terms switch off turns an opaque coefficient into something you can check by eye.

Why raw powers destroy precision

A computer stores each number in double precision: about 16 significant digits, and no more.

$20^{10}=1.024\times10^{13},\qquad \text{machine precision }\epsilon\approx 2.2\times10^{-16}$

Analogy: imagine a tape measure that always shows 16 digits total. If the first 13 digits are spent on kilometres, only 3 digits are left for millimetres. When the same matrix mixes 1 with $10^{13}$, the small entries are working with the leftover digits.

PathCondition numberDigits left of the 16
Raw $x$, design matrix $X$1.02×10¹³≈ 3
Raw $x$, normal equations $X^\top X$1.03×10²⁶none — worse than the noise floor
Rescaled $x/20$, design matrix3.74×10³≈ 12
Read the middle row again. Forming $X^\top X$ squares the condition number. At $10^{26}$ the matrix is, to the computer, numerically singular: the answer you get back is noise wearing a suit. This is why we will never write inv(X.T @ X) in this task.

What a condition number means

Forget the formula for a second. A condition number answers one question:

"If I nudge the input by 0.0001%, how much can the answer move?" A condition number of $10^{13}$ means: up to $10^{13}$ times as much. Relative input error gets amplified by that factor.

Two mechanical pictures. A well-conditioned problem is a bicycle: turn the handlebars a little, the wheel turns a little. An ill-conditioned problem is a wobbly shopping trolley: nudge it a hair and it veers into the shelves.

Formally it is the ratio of the largest to the smallest singular value of $X$ — how much the matrix stretches its most-stretched direction versus its least:

$\kappa(X)=\dfrac{d_{\max}}{d_{\min}}$

The crimson staircase falls off a cliff; the teal one barely slopes. Same data, same model, different units.

Rescaling is mandatory, not optional

The fix costs one line of code and buys back nine orders of magnitude.

$t=\dfrac{x}{\max|x|}=\dfrac{x}{20}\in[-1,1] \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \kappa:\ 1.02\times10^{13}\ \longrightarrow\ 3.74\times10^{3}$
BeforeAfterImprovement
$\kappa(X)=1.02\times10^{13}$$\kappa(X)=3.74\times10^{3}$factor 2.7×10⁹

Why it works: on $[-1,1]$ every power $t^{j}$ also lives in $[-1,1]$, so no column dwarfs any other. On $[-19,20]$ the columns differ by 13 decades.

Rescale $y$ too

$\tilde y=\dfrac{y}{\max|y|}=\dfrac{y}{5.98\times10^{13}}$

Now the whole problem is dimensionless, and that matters for one specific reason: the ridge penalty compares $\lVert y-X\alpha\rVert^2$ against $\lambda\lVert\alpha\rVert^2$, adding a squared response to a squared coefficient. Those only live on the same scale if both have been standardised.

Therefore $\lambda$ is only meaningful on the standardised problem. A $\lambda$ quoted on raw data means nothing to a reader — and a $\lambda$ transplanted from raw to scaled data will be wrong by many orders of magnitude. Always say which problem your $\lambda$ belongs to. Transform coefficients back at the end if you want to report the polynomial in original units.

Solve by SVD, never by inverting $X^\top X$

Textbooks write the normal equations solution, and it is algebraically right:

$\hat\alpha_{\text{OLS}}=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$

But we saw it: $\kappa(X^\top X)=\kappa(X)^2=1.03\times10^{26}$. Squaring an already-bad condition number is how you turn a hard problem into an impossible one. Use the singular value decomposition (SVD) instead — it works on $X$ directly and never forms the squared matrix.

$X=UDV^\top,\qquad \hat\alpha_{\text{OLS}}=V\,D^{-1}U^\top y=\sum_{j}\frac{u_j^\top y}{d_j}\,v_j$

Read the sum: each direction $v_j$ in coefficient space gets a contribution divided by its singular value $d_j$. Directions the data barely constrains (tiny $d_j$) get divided by almost nothing, so they blow up. That single division is the entire instability of OLS here, and it is exactly what ridge will fix.

In practice: numpy.linalg.lstsq (which uses SVD internally) or numpy.linalg.svd explicitly. Name the solver in the workbook — the graders want to see that the numerical route was a deliberate choice.

Ridge: the same fit, on a leash

OLS has exactly one instruction: make the residuals small. Nothing stops it using enormous coefficients that cancel each other out. Ridge adds a second instruction: and keep the coefficients small.

$\hat\alpha_\lambda=\arg\min_{\alpha}\ \Big(\underbrace{\lVert y-X\alpha\rVert_2^{2}}_{\text{fit the data}}+\underbrace{\lambda\lVert\alpha\rVert_2^{2}}_{\text{stay small}}\Big)$

Picture a spring attached from each coefficient to zero. $\lambda$ is the stiffness. Weak spring: the coefficients go wherever the data pulls them (OLS). Stiff spring: they are dragged toward zero and the curve flattens out.

$\lambda$What happensFailure mode
$0$Exactly OLSWild, unstable coefficients (variance)
moderateSlightly worse fit, far smaller coefficients— the sweet spot
$\to\infty$Coefficients collapse to zero, curve flattensSystematically wrong (bias)
Two different reasons people reach for ridge, often conflated: (1) the design matrix is ill-conditioned or singular and OLS has no stable answer — that is our situation; (2) there are more predictors than observations — not our situation (22 > 11). Say which one you mean.

Ridge through the SVD lens

Same decomposition, one character changed in the denominator — and that character is the whole idea.

$\hat\alpha_{\text{OLS}}=\sum_j \frac{u_j^\top y}{d_j}v_j \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad \hat\alpha_\lambda=\sum_j \frac{d_j}{d_j^{2}+\lambda}\,(u_j^\top y)\,v_j$

In matrix form, the version to implement:

$\hat\alpha_\lambda=V\,\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\frac{d_j}{d_j^{2}+\lambda}\right)U^\top y$

The factor $d_j^2/(d_j^2+\lambda)$ is a shrinkage filter applied direction by direction:

  • $d_j^2 \gg \lambda$ → factor ≈ 1: well-determined directions pass through untouched.
  • $d_j^2 \ll \lambda$ → factor ≈ 0: the barely-visible directions, the ones that blow up in OLS, get switched off.
So ridge is not a blunt "shrink everything" instrument. It shrinks selectively, and it shrinks hardest exactly where the data has least to say. That is why it stabilises an ill-conditioned problem instead of merely damaging it.
Do not penalise the intercept. Shrinking $\alpha_0$ toward zero means asserting the curve should pass near the origin — an arbitrary claim that depends on where you happened to put the zero of your $y$ axis. Standard practice: centre the ten power columns, keep the intercept out of the penalty, and recover it as $\hat\alpha_0=\bar{\tilde y}-\bar{x}^\top\hat\beta$.

Choosing λ by cross-validation

$\lambda$ cannot be chosen by minimising the training error — that always picks $\lambda=0$, i.e. OLS, by construction. We need a proxy for performance on data the fit has not seen.

Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV)

  1. Hide point 1. Fit ridge with penalty $\lambda$ on the other 21. Predict point 1. Record the error.
  2. Repeat for all 22 points.
  3. The root mean square of those 22 held-out errors is the LOOCV score for that $\lambda$.
  4. Sweep $\lambda$ on a logarithmic grid and take the minimum.
Result: $\lambda^\star \approx 4.47\times10^{-4}$ on the standardised problem. The valley is shallow — anything from roughly $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-3}$ scores about the same. Say so in the workbook: a flat optimum means the exact digits of $\lambda^\star$ do not matter, which is a much more honest claim than quoting six decimals.

With $n=22$, leave-one-out is 22 tiny fits — essentially free. On a bigger dataset you would use 5- or 10-fold cross-validation for speed.

OLS wins on in-sample error — as it must. OLS is by definition the minimiser of that exact quantity, so no ridge fit can ever beat it there. Reporting "OLS has the lowest training error" is therefore not a finding; it is arithmetic. The interesting column is the next one.

Ridge cuts the coefficient norm from 6.63 to 1.007 — an 84.8% reduction — while the in-sample RMSE grows by a factor of only 1.8. Push to $\lambda=10^{-2}$ and you buy a further 30% shrinkage for a 3.4× worse fit: clearly past the point of diminishing returns, which is what makes $\lambda^\star$ defensible.

Reading the table: bias vs variance

Every prediction error decomposes into three pieces. Two of them we can trade against each other.

$\mathbb{E}\big[(y-\hat f(x))^{2}\big]=\underbrace{\text{bias}^{2}}_{\text{systematically off}}+\underbrace{\text{variance}}_{\text{jumps with the sample}}+\underbrace{\sigma^{2}}_{\text{irreducible noise}}$
BiasVarianceIn-sample fit

The bargain: ridge accepts a little systematic error in exchange for a solution that would barely move if you re-measured the data. On a problem with $\kappa=10^{13}$ that is an excellent trade — an unbiased estimator whose value is dominated by noise is not useful, however unbiased it is.

Lookalike alert — OLS vs ridge. They are not "two competing models". They are the same model (degree-10 polynomial) fitted with two different objectives. OLS optimises fit alone; ridge optimises fit plus stability. Choosing between them is choosing what you want the fit for: OLS if you only ever describe these 22 points, ridge if any number will be reused, extrapolated, or interpreted.

Where both solutions fail badly

This is the answer to "what are the qualities of each of the solutions?" — and it is not flattering to either.

Least squares minimises absolute squared error, so the fit is bought and paid for by the handful of points where $|y|\approx 6\times10^{13}$. In the region $|x|\le 7$, where the true values never exceed $1.8\times10^{9}$, neither fit is worth anything:

Both fits are worthless on the interior. Ridge improves matters roughly fiftyfold but does not fix the cause: it penalises coefficient size, not error relative to the local magnitude of $y$.
SUB-CHAPTER

If the small-x region mattered

The failure on slide 15 is not a bug in ridge or in the solver. It is the loss function doing precisely what it was told: absolute squared error says an error of $10^{9}$ at $x=0$ is negligible, because it is negligible compared with $10^{13}$.

So if the interior mattered, the criterion — not the estimator — is what to change:

ApproachMinimiseEffect
Relative error$\sum\big((y_i-\hat y_i)/y_i\big)^{2}$Every point counts equally in percentage terms
Weighted least squares$\sum w_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^{2}$ with $w_i=1/y_i^{2}$Same idea, standard machinery, easy to defend
Log-scale fit$\sum\big(\log|y_i|-\log|\hat y_i|\big)^{2}$Natural here: all $y<0$, and $\log|y|$ is nearly linear in $|x|$
Say this in the workbook, but do not substitute it for the requested task. The exam asks for OLS and ridge on squared error — deliver exactly that, then add the remark. Diagnosing why a correctly-executed method gives a useless answer in part of the domain is the difference between running a recipe and understanding it.

Do not reweight silently either. Weighted least squares answers a different question from the one asked; changing the question without flagging it is the kind of thing that makes a workbook untrustworthy.

Quest complete

Quick self-check before the notebook

Q1. Why can we not choose $\lambda$ by minimising the fitted residual sum of squares?
Because that quantity is minimised at $\lambda=0$ by definition — it would always select OLS. $\lambda$ has to be judged on data the fit has not seen.

Q2. Rescaling $x$ to $[-1,1]$ does not change the fitted curve at all. So why bother?
Mathematically it is the same fit; numerically it is the difference between 3 reliable digits and 12. It also makes $\lambda$ interpretable.

Q3. Ridge has larger in-sample error than OLS. In what sense is it then "better"?
It is not better at describing these 22 points — it cannot be. It is better at being reproducible: its coefficient vector is 84.8% smaller and would barely move under re-measurement, whereas the OLS solution is dominated by directions the data barely constrains.

Q4. What single number best exposes the weakness of both fits?
The fitted value at $x=0$: OLS says $+1.90\times10^{11}$, the data says $-0.98$.
