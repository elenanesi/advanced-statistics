CAUSAL INFERENCE · CONCEPT DECK

CAUSAL IMPACT

Bayes: Revenue went up after the campaign. But would it have gone up anyway? That's the whole quest.

This deck covers:

  • What causal impact actually measures
  • Why A/B tests aren't always possible
  • How BSTS builds a Bayesian counterfactual
  • How to choose covariates safely
  • Multi-method validation & permutation tests
  • Real case: a UK retailer's delivery promo, fully validated

THE CORE QUESTION

Any analyst can report a before/after number. The hard part is:

Causal Effect = What actually happened  −  What would have happened anyway

The second term — the counterfactual — is never observed. You have to construct it.

WHY THIS IS HARD

  • Revenue in March is up 20% vs. February
  • But March is seasonally stronger every year
  • And Google ran a promo and it was payday weekend
  • Which part was really the campaign?
► Naive before/after = "attribution theater". You need a model of the world without the intervention.

THE A/B TEST IDEAL

Ridge: Randomised groups → identical in expectation → difference = pure causal effect. But you need two groups that can exist simultaneously.
SituationA/B Test?Why
Promo email to 50% of users ✔ YES Two segments, same time
Price change on product page △ HARD Network effects, price perception spillover
New website layout — all users ✘ NO 100% rollout — no control group exists
Bank changes its UX for all customers ✘ NO No comparable segment without the change
TV/OOH campaign in all regions ✘ NO No untreated region to compare
► When A/B testing is impossible, you need to construct the missing control from historical patterns. That's what Causal Impact does.

REAL CASE: RETAIL PROMO

ANONYMIZED · UK E-COMMERCE RETAILER

THE INTERVENTION

A UK online retailer ran a 4-day delivery discount promo. Goal: measure whether it generated incremental revenue beyond what would have happened anyway.

THE ANALYSIS PIPELINE

StepWhat happened
Pre-period 51 days of post-Christmas data (homogeneous regime) — chosen over 787-day full history because shorter = less seasonal noise
Covariates Day-of-week, holidays, weather, Google Trends, sale-period detection. Paid sessions flagged as contaminated (media spend responded to the promo) and excluded.
Methods 6 methods run: BSTS (VI + HMC), CausalPy, RDiT, Prophet, Conformal CIs. All agreed the effect was positive.
Validation 448-spec Specification Curve Analysis. 99.5% of model variants showed a positive effect (range: +154K to +408K).
Permutation test perm-p = 0.005 — the real promo effect was larger than 99.5% of random placebo dates.

KEY LESSONS LEARNED

  • LESSON 1 Including paid sessions as a covariate inflated model p from 0.04 to 0.19 — the covariate was absorbing the effect.
  • LESSON 2 Pre-period length can fake significance. Two runs of the same promo, same methods — only the pre-period changed:
    • 787-day pre-period (full history): model p = 0.020 looked “significant” — but perm-p = 0.47 (almost half of random placebo promo dates looked just as good). Do not trust this run.
    • 51-day clean pre-period (post-Christmas only, one seasonal regime): perm-p = 0.005 — the real effect beat 99.5% of placebo dates. Trust this run.
    ► Why the mismatch? A long pre-period mixes Black Friday, Christmas, summer lulls, and normal weeks. The model can fit that mash-up and emit a low model p-value — but so can many fake intervention dates. The permutation test (perm-p) asks: “If I pretend the promo started on random days, how often do I get a lift this big?” With 787 days, often (~47%). With 51 homogeneous days, almost never (0.5%).
    What are model p and perm-p?

    Model p — p-value from the Bayesian Structural Time Series (BSTS) fit: “Is zero inside the credible interval for the cumulative effect?” Often too optimistic (miscalibrated).

    Perm-p — permutation test p-value: shuffle many fake treatment start dates on the same pre-period, re-run the analysis, count how often |placebo effect| ≥ |real effect|. Low perm-p = the real promo beat almost all noise dates.

    Concrete rule: If model p looks good but perm-p > 0.20, treat the result as unvalidated — shorten or clean the pre-period until perm-p ≤ 0.10.

  • LESSON 3 Multi-method convergence: conservative estimate (RDiT) ~160K, primary (BSTS HMC) ~300K, upper (Prophet) ~355K. All positive, direction unanimous.

BSTS: BUILD THE COUNTERFACTUAL

Bayes: Learn the metric's normal behavior from the pre-period. Then ask: what would it have been? The gap is the causal effect.

THE THREE PHASES

① PRE-PERIOD — Train a structural time-series model on historical data. Capture: local level, weekly seasonality, holiday effects, correlated signals (covariates).
② COUNTERFACTUAL — Extend the model forward through the intervention period as if nothing happened. Produces a posterior predictive distribution (not just a line — full uncertainty).
③ CAUSAL EFFECT — Observed minus counterfactual. Credible interval tells you if zero is plausible.
α(t) = y(t) − ŷ(t)  ← daily lift (intensity on day t)
Cumulative: Σ α(t) over treatment window  ← total campaign lift
► This is how Causal Impact answers “effect over time”: not one slope-break like segmented ITS regression, but a pointwise impact for every post-intervention day (plus the cumulative sum). See causal-models deck · INTERRUPTED TIME SERIES for the full flavor map.

COVARIATES: ALLY & ENEMY

Covariates help the model explain variance that isn't the campaign. They make the counterfactual sharper — but only if they're safe.

GOOD COVARIATES — INCLUDE

  • ✔ SAFE Seasonality signals: day-of-week, holidays, payday proximity
  • ✔ SAFE Exogenous demand: weather, Google Trends (category)
  • ✔ SAFE Unaffected channels: organic sessions (if promo didn't drive organic traffic)
  • ✔ SAFE Competitor signals / macro indicators

CONTAMINATED COVARIATES — EXCLUDE

A covariate that itself changed because of the intervention will absorb the causal effect — making a real effect look like zero.
  • ✘ SKIP Paid sessions — if media spend responded to the promo, paid traffic went up too
  • ✘ SKIP Email open rate — triggered by the campaign, not exogenous
  • ✘ SKIP Any metric downstream of the treatment
Contamination check: | covariatetreatment − covariatelast 7 days pre | > 10% → flag as CAUTION / SKIP

NEVER TRUST ONE METHOD

Ridge: Every method has assumptions. If they all agree → convergent validity. If they disagree → investigate why before reporting anything.
MethodWhat it testsBest for
BSTS / tfcausalimpact Bayesian posterior counterfactual (VI or HMC) Long campaigns, rich covariates
CausalPy (ITS) Bayesian interrupted time-series regression Structural breaks, medium series
RDiT Regression Discontinuity in Time Short campaigns (≤2 weeks), sharp cut-off
Prophet Additive seasonality cross-validation Seasonal model sanity check
► If BSTS says +22%, RDiT says +18%, and CausalPy says +19% → direction and magnitude agree. Report with confidence. If one says −5%, that's a signal to investigate assumptions, not to average.

PERMUTATION TESTS

DON'T TRUST THE MODEL P-VALUE

THE PROBLEM WITH BSTS P-VALUES (GENERIC)

False Positive Rate (FPR) — generic property of BSTS-class tools, not one client: on placebo runs (fake intervention dates, no real campaign), built-in p-values still say “significant” about 35–55% of the time. So model p=0.02 does not by itself prove the effect is real.
What is a placebo run?

Keep your data. Pick a random day in the pre-period and pretend that was launch day. Run Causal Impact. If “significant” results appear often when nothing happened, the toolbox is miscalibrated — you need the permutation check on the real launch date below.

THE PERMUTATION TEST LOGIC

  • Take your pre-period data
  • Assign random fake treatment dates (many times)
  • Run the same analysis on each shuffle
  • Ask: how often does the random date produce an effect as large as the real one?
Permutation p = (# shuffles with |effect| ≥ |real effect| + 1) / (N shuffles + 1)

THE VERDICT THRESHOLD

  • perm-p ≤ 0.10 → validated result, report with confidence
  • perm-p 0.10–0.20 → directional signal, uncertain significance
  • perm-p > 0.20 → not statistically distinguishable from noise
► Worked example (illustrates the generic trap): one retail spec had model p=0.020 but permutation p=0.47 with an over-long pre-period; a cleaner short pre-period gave perm-p=0.009. Same method, different data choices — not “retail is different.”

WATCH OUT!

Hootsworth: I've seen analysts report p=0.02 with a perm-p of 0.47. The model looked significant. It wasn't. Here's what trips people up.
  • TRAP 1 Contaminated covariate — including paid_sessions when media spend responded to the promo. Effect gets absorbed → p inflates to 0.18.
  • TRAP 2 Pre-period too long — including multiple Black Fridays, Christmas peaks. Seasonal noise swamps the signal. Homogeneous pre-period beats long pre-period.
  • TRAP 3 Model p ≠ permutation p — BSTS VI is miscalibrated. Always run permutation tests before reporting significance.
  • TRAP 4 Mask zero-injection bug — masking a period then re-running data prep can silently fill masked days with y=0 + interpolated covariates. Verify your masked data has no rows, not rows of zeros.
  • TRAP 5 Cherry-picking specs — reporting only the pre-period mode with lowest p-value. Show the full specification curve across all analytical choices.

WHICH METHOD? DECISION GUIDE

SituationRecommended approach
Randomised groups possible, same time period A/B Test — gold standard, use it
100% rollout, no control group, >3 months pre-data BSTS + permutation tfcausalimpact or CausalPy ITS
Short campaign ≤2 weeks, sharp start date RDiT — local bandwidth, less pre-period needed
Geo-level data, some regions treated / some not Synthetic Control / GeoLift
100% rollout, single time series (no holdout) BSTS + RDiT both, cross-validate. Add permutation test.
Less than 3× campaign length in pre-data Underpowered — report MDE, do not claim causal significance

MINIMUM REQUIREMENTS FOR BSTS

  • Pre-period ≥ 3× campaign length (e.g. 15-day promo → 45+ days pre)
  • Homogeneous pre-period — same seasonal regime as treatment window
  • At least one exogenous covariate for variance reduction
  • Permutation test run & reported alongside model p-value

  • Causal effect = observed minus counterfactual. The counterfactual must be modelled, not assumed.
  • When A/B tests are impossible (100% rollouts, no control group), BSTS + RDiT construct the counterfactual from the pre-period time series.
  • Covariates sharpen the model — but contaminated covariates absorb the effect. Run the safety audit.
  • Model p-values are miscalibrated (35–55% FPR). Always validate with permutation tests. Report permutation p, not model p.
  • Never rely on a single method. Cross-validate with BSTS, RDiT, CausalPy, Prophet. Convergence = confidence.
  • Specification Curve Analysis: test all analytical choices, report the full distribution — not the cherry-picked winner.
