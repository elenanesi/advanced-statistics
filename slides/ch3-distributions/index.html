The one idea of chapter 3

A distribution is not a formula to memorize. It's a pre-solved answer to a question about randomness. Someone already worked out "if the world behaves like this, then the outcomes spread out like that" — and gave the answer a name.

So the skill this chapter teaches is not algebra. It's recognition: which question am I actually asking?

Counting things (discrete)
"How many wins in n tries?" → BINOMIAL
"How many events per hour / per day / per m²?" → POISSON
"How many losses before my k-th win?" → NEGATIVE BINOMIAL
Measuring things (continuous)
"What does the sum of many small random effects look like?" → NORMAL
"How long until the next event?" → EXPONENTIAL
"When will this thing break?" → WEIBULL
"I already know the distribution of X — what's the distribution of a function of X?" → TRANSFORMATION THEOREM (section 3.5)

Left column counts (0, 1, 2, …) — those use a PMF. Right column measures (any decimal) — those use a PDF. Same split you met in the intro deck.

Binomial — how many wins in n tries?

Concrete first. You run an A/B test: 10 visitors see the new checkout button. Each visitor independently clicks (probability $p$) or doesn't. Question: how many of the 10 click? That count is a binomial random variable.

Each single visitor is a Bernoulli trial — exactly the coin from your Task 1 deck. Binomial = n independent Bernoulli trials, then count the successes.

$$P(X=k)=\binom{n}{k}\,p^{k}\,(1-p)^{n-k}$$ Read right to left: $(1-p)^{n-k}$ = the losses happen · $p^k$ = the wins happen · $\binom{n}{k}$ = how many orderings produce that mix (next slide).

Mean and variance: $E[X]=np$ and $V[X]=np(1-p)$. The mean needs no algebra: each trial contributes $p$ on average (Task 1 result!), and independent contributions just add — 10 visitors × 0.3 click-rate = 3 expected clicks.

Book's worked example: probability of exactly 5 heads in 10 fair tosses $=\binom{10}{5}0.5^5\,0.5^5 \approx 0.246$. Only ~25% — even the single most likely outcome is far from certain.

SUB-CHAPTER

Why the "n choose k" term exists

Take 4 coin tosses, ask for exactly 2 heads. Each specific sequence with 2 heads has the same probability $p^2(1-p)^2$ — multiplication doesn't care about order:

$$\binom{n}{k}=\frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!} \qquad\quad \binom{4}{2}=\frac{24}{2\cdot 2}=6$$

The binomial coefficient is just that count of arrangements. So the binomial PMF is literally: (probability of one qualifying sequence) × (number of qualifying sequences). Nothing more mysterious than that.

Negative binomial — flip the question

Binomial fixes the number of tries and asks about wins. The negative binomial does the mirror image: fix the number of wins you need, ask how many losses you'll suffer along the way.

Concrete: you're cold-calling clients and need $k=3$ signed deals before you can stop. Each call succeeds with probability $p$. How many rejections $m$ pile up before deal number 3?

$$P(M=m)=\binom{m+k-1}{k-1}\,p^{k}\,(1-p)^{m} \qquad E[M]=\frac{k(1-p)}{p}$$ The coefficient counts arrangements of the first $m+k-1$ slots only — the final success is pinned.

The mean passes the sanity check: with $p=0.5$ and $k=3$ deals, expect $3\cdot 0.5/0.5 = 3$ rejections — one failure per success, on average. The odd name comes from an algebra identity that writes the coefficient with a negative top number; nothing about the distribution is negative.

Remember this creature — it re-appears in section 3.3 through a completely different door.

Normal — the pile-up law

Why does one bell curve rule height, BMI, measurement errors, everything? Because of one mechanism: when many small independent random effects add up, their sum always piles into the same bell shape — no matter what each little effect looks like. That's the Central Limit Theorem (CLT).

The book's picture for this is the Galton board: a marble falls through rows of pegs, bouncing left or right at each one — every peg is a tiny Bernoulli trial. The final slot = the sum of all the bounces:

$$f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\;e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$$ Two knobs only: $\mu$ slides the center, $\sigma$ sets the width. Notation: $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$.
The CLT stated properly (book box, p. 73)

If $X_1,\dots,X_n$ are i.i.d. with mean $\bar{X}$ and variance $\sigma^2$, then the sum $W=\sum_i X_i$ converges toward a normal distribution with mean $n\bar{X}$ and variance $n\sigma^2$ as $n$ grows.

The σ ruler — reading "±" honestly

When a paper reports "BMI in young Italian men: 23.05 ± 2.83" (the book's real example), it's claiming a normal distribution with $\mu = 23.05$, $\sigma = 2.83$. The ± is a ruler marking, not a guarantee:

The gotcha the book flags: a man with BMI 26.38 sits just outside +1σ. Not an outlier, not an error — you should expect ~32% of people outside the ±1σ band. The book goes further: if a data set shows fewer than ~32% of values outside its reported ± range, be suspicious of how the results were reported.

Memorize the ruler once: 68 / 95 / 99.7 for 1σ / 2σ / 3σ. This becomes the vocabulary of Task 4 — a "significant" result is just "too many σ away from the boring explanation".

Poisson — counts of pure randomness

Concrete first. A supermarket sells a product on average 5 times per day. Sales happen at random moments, independently. How likely is it that tomorrow sells 0? 3? 9? That's the Poisson question: how many events land in one unit of time (or space), when events are individually unpredictable but arrive at a known average rate?

$$P(X=k)=\frac{\lambda^{k}e^{-\lambda}}{k!} \qquad\qquad E[X]=V[X]=\lambda$$

The defining quirk: one single knob. Choose the mean and you have automatically chosen the variance — they're handcuffed together, both equal to $\lambda$. This is elegant and it's the distribution's weakness, which section 3.3 exploits two slides from now.

SUB-CHAPTER

Poisson = binomial pushed to the limit

Where does that formula come from? Chop the day into 86,400 seconds. In each second, a sale either happens (tiny probability $p$) or doesn't. That's 86,400 Bernoulli trials → the daily count is binomial with huge $n$ and tiny $p$.

Push $n\to\infty$ while holding the mean $np=\lambda$ fixed, and the binomial PMF collapses into the Poisson PMF — the book proves it on p. 77 by showing $\binom{n}{k}\frac{1}{n^k}\to\frac{1}{k!}$ and $\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^n\to e^{-\lambda}$.

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\binom{n}{k}\left(\tfrac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{k}\left(1-\tfrac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{n-k}=\frac{\lambda^{k}e^{-\lambda}}{k!}$$

Bonus fact from the same section: the sum of independent Poisson counts is again Poisson, with $\lambda = \lambda_1+\lambda_2$. Two stores selling at rates 5 and 3 per day → combined sales are Poisson with rate 8. Exam-relevant reflex: some families are closed under addition (Poisson, Normal, Gamma) — your Task 3 uses exactly this for the Gamma.

Gamma-Poisson — when counts wobble more

The book fits real supermarket sales with a Poisson and it almost works — but the data's variance is bigger than its mean. The Poisson physically cannot draw that shape: its one knob forces variance = mean. Real counts are usually overdispersed.

Why? Because "average 5 sales/day" is a fiction — the true rate wobbles: rainy Tuesdays, paydays, promotions. So model the wobble: let $\lambda$ itself be a random variable, drawn from a Gamma distribution, and only then draw the Poisson count:

$$X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda), \qquad \lambda \sim \text{Gamma}(r, \beta) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad X \sim \text{NB}(r, p)$$ Working the mixture integral out (book pp. 81–82), the answer is exactly the negative binomial from slide 4 — the same creature, entered through a different door.

Practical recipe (book eq. 3.27–3.28): from your data's mean and variance, set $p = \mu/\sigma^2$ and $r = \mu^2/(\sigma^2-\mu)$. If $\sigma^2 = \mu$, just use Poisson.

Where you'll meet the Gamma again (Tasks 3 & 6)

The Gamma density used here, $f_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=\frac{\beta^{\alpha}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}$, is the star of your Task 3 (router traffic, Gamma(7, θ) — see slides/task3-gamma-mle). And "Gamma is the conjugate prior of the Poisson" is the exact machinery of Task 6. The mixture idea on this slide is Bayesian statistics quietly introducing itself.

Exponential — how long until the next event?

Same random process as Poisson — support calls arriving at rate $\lambda$ per hour — but flip the question: not "how many calls this hour?" but "how long do I wait for the next one?" Counts were discrete; waiting time is continuous. New question, new distribution.

The derivation is one honest line — my favorite in the whole chapter. "My wait exceeds $t$" means exactly "zero events happened in $[0,t]$", and Poisson already knows that probability:

$$P(L>t)=P(\text{0 events in } t)=\frac{(\lambda t)^0 e^{-\lambda t}}{0!}=e^{-\lambda t}$$ Flip to the CDF: $P(L\le t)=1-e^{-\lambda t}$, take the derivative, and out falls the density: $$f(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}, \quad t>0$$

That "peak at now, then decay" shape should feel familiar: your Task 2 survival function $\bar{F}(y)$ is built on exactly this kind of decay, and $P(L>t)=e^{-\lambda t}$ is a survival function — the probability the wait "survives" past $t$.

Weibull — when do things break?

The exponential has a hidden, often false assumption: the event's risk never changes — a 10-year-old turbine is as likely to fail today as a new one. Real machines age. Real electronics have factory defects that surface early. The Weibull distribution fixes this with one extra knob, the shape parameter $k$, which controls how the failure rate evolves over time:

$$f(x;\lambda,k)=\frac{k}{\lambda}\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)^{k-1}e^{-(x/\lambda)^{k}}, \quad x\ge 0$$ $k$ = shape (how risk changes) · $\lambda$ = scale (typical lifetime). At $k=1$ it collapses to $\lambda e^{-x/\lambda}$ — the exponential is a Weibull special case.
  • $k<1$ — infant mortality: most failures early (factory defects); survivors get safer over time.
  • $k=1$ — pure randomness: constant risk, no memory. Exponential.
  • $k>1$ — aging: few early failures, then wear-out. The turbine story: schedule maintenance before the hump.

The business case (book): predictive maintenance = use sensor data to estimate a component's Weibull parameters, then service it just before its failure probability crosses your risk threshold — instead of on a rigid calendar.

Transformed variables — new lens, same photo

Section 3.5 answers the "meta" question: you know the distribution of $X$ — what's the distribution of a function of it, $g(X)$? E.g. you model server response time $X$, but the dashboard reports $\log(X)$; or Task 2, where you know $F(y)$ and need the density of a transformed variable.

Intuition: probability is area, and area must survive the stretch. Transforming the axis is like printing the density on a rubber sheet and stretching it. Where the axis stretches ×2, the curve must flatten ÷2 — otherwise probability would be created from nothing:

One dimension: $$f_{g(X)}(u)=f_X\!\left(g^{-1}(u)\right)\cdot\left|\,(g^{-1})'(u)\,\right|$$ Read it as: go back to the original point ($g^{-1}$), look up the old density there, correct for the local stretch (the derivative's absolute value).

In several dimensions the "local stretch factor" becomes the Jacobian determinant — same idea, matrix edition. The book's worked example transforms two independent Gamma variables via $g(x,y)=(x-y,\,x+y)$ to get the density of a difference. You have already used this theorem twice: Task 2 (chain-rule PDF) and Task 3 (the convolution proof). Section 3.5 is just the theorem those decks borrowed, in its official home.

Watch out! — the classic traps

  • Poisson vs exponential mixups. Same process, different question: Poisson counts events per unit (discrete), exponential measures the wait between them (continuous). If the answer is "3 calls" it's Poisson; if it's "12 minutes" it's exponential.
  • "±1σ covers most of the data." No — 68%, with a full third outside. The book explicitly flags this as the mistake practitioners make when reading "23.05 ± 2.83".
  • Fitting Poisson to wobbly counts. First compute mean and variance of your data. Variance ≈ mean → Poisson. Variance > mean → negative binomial. Poisson literally cannot represent the second case.
  • Same letter, different jobs. $k$ is a success count in 3.1, an event count in 3.3, and a shape parameter in 3.4. $\lambda$ is a rate in Poisson/exponential but a scale in Weibull. Read the distribution's name before reading its symbols.
  • "Density > 1 must be an error." No — look at the exponential chart: $\lambda = 1.5$ starts at height 1.5. Densities are not probabilities; only their areas are. (Your PMF-vs-PDF instinct from the intro deck, now with visible proof.)
  • Negative binomial conventions differ. Books and libraries disagree on whether it counts failures or total trials, and whether $p$ is success or failure probability. In scipy.stats.nbinom(n, p), check the docstring against eq. 3.6 before trusting numbers — same trust-but-verify reflex as the scipy scale-vs-rate trap in Task 3.

Quiz — pick the creature

1. A support hotline receives on average 6 calls per hour, arriving at random. Which distribution describes (a) the number of calls in the next hour, (b) the minutes until the next call?

Reveal answer

(a) Poisson with $\lambda = 6$ — counting events per unit time. (b) Exponential with rate 6 per hour — waiting time between events. Same process, two questions.

2. Your daily sales counts have mean 15 and variance 50. Poisson or negative binomial?

Reveal answer

Negative binomial — variance (50) > mean (15), i.e. overdispersed, and Poisson forces variance = mean. Parameters via eq. 3.27–3.28: $p = 15/50 = 0.3$, $r = 225/35 \approx 6.43$.

3. A component almost never fails before month 15 but failures cluster around month 20. Weibull shape: $k<1$, $k=1$, or $k>1$?

Reveal answer

$k > 1$ (and large): failure risk grows with age and the density is localized late — the wear-out regime. Schedule maintenance just before the hump.

4. A study reports reaction time 310 ± 40 ms (normal). Roughly what share of people fall outside 270–350 ms?

Reveal answer

About 32% — that band is exactly ±1σ, which contains only ~68%. If a data set claims far fewer outside, the book says: be suspicious of the reporting.

5. You know the density of $X$ and need the density of $Y = 3X$. What two ingredients does the transformation theorem ask for?

Reveal answer

The inverse transformation ($g^{-1}(y) = y/3$) and its derivative's absolute value ($1/3$): $f_Y(y) = f_X(y/3)\cdot\tfrac{1}{3}$. The axis stretched ×3, so the density flattens ÷3 — area preserved.

Quest complete

Penny: Field guide complete! Next time a problem says "how many…" or "how long until…", you'll hear the distribution announce itself before any formula appears.
  • A distribution is a pre-solved answer to a question — matching the question is the real skill.
  • Binomial counts wins in n fixed tries; negative binomial fixes the wins and counts the losses.
  • The Normal is what sums of many small random effects always become — that's the CLT, and the σ ruler reads 68 / 95 / 99.7.
  • Poisson counts pure randomness with one knob (mean = variance); when real counts wobble more, let λ be Gamma → negative binomial.
  • Exponential = the wait between Poisson events (peak risk always "now"); Weibull adds a shape knob for infant mortality vs aging.
  • Transforming a variable stretches its axis; the density compensates by the |derivative| (1-D) or Jacobian (n-D) so probability-area survives.
